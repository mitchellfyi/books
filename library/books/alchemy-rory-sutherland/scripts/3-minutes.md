Alchemy, by Rory Sutherland, is about why sensible-looking solutions often fail when people are involved, while strange ideas sometimes work.

Sutherland is an advertising executive who helped build Ogilvy's behavioural consulting practice. His central argument is that logic is necessary but incomplete. Businesses tend to measure price, speed, size, and efficiency, then assume people choose by comparing those features. Real decisions also depend on context, expectation, trust, identity, status, and emotion. Sutherland calls the hidden reasoning behind those choices psycho-logic.

The book opens with Red Bull. A rational product plan might propose a cheaper, better-tasting drink in a larger can. Red Bull succeeded with a costly, small can and a taste many people disliked. The odd combination created its own meaning.

Several ideas explain effects like this. Signalling means visible cost, effort, or form can communicate a hidden quality such as commitment or confidence. Removing an apparently inefficient feature can therefore remove the reason people trusted an offer. Self-signalling means choices also tell us who we are, reinforcing identity and future behaviour.

Satisficing explains why people rarely calculate a perfect option. With limited time and information, they use shortcuts and choose something acceptable. Psychophysics adds that physical and perceived changes are not proportional. Reliable countdown information, for example, may improve a wait more than a modest reduction in its actual length because uncertainty was the real source of discomfort.

The argument also matters inside organisations. Managers can defend a familiar proposal with a forecast, while an original proposal looks irresponsible until it produces results. This rewards conformity even when every competitor uses the same assumptions. Sutherland's answer is not blind intuition. It is a portfolio of explanations and low-cost trials, judged by observed behaviour. In that sense, the book defends empirical curiosity against premature certainty, although its own cases do not always meet the evidential standard it recommends.

The practical lesson is to widen the solution space. Do not design only for an imaginary average user. Generate several explanations, notice small sources of anxiety or meaning, and test cheap, reversible ideas that competitors dismiss. Counterintuitive does not mean correct; it means the idea should not be rejected before evidence exists.

Readers praise Alchemy for its humour, memorable examples, and challenge to rigid corporate thinking. Critics find it repetitive and loosely organised. They also argue that Sutherland sometimes caricatures economists, uses anecdotes where stronger evidence is needed, and offers limited guidance on when an effect will generalise.

The book suits marketers, designers, entrepreneurs, and policy practitioners who can run real experiments. It is not a behavioural-science textbook, systematic evidence review, or step-by-step playbook. Read it to generate better questions and neglected hypotheses. Look elsewhere if you mainly want formal models, effect sizes, or a compact framework.
