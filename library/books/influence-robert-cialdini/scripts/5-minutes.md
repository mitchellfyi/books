In the mid nineteen-seventies, a social psychology professor got tired of being an easy mark. Robert Cialdini bought things he did not want, gave to causes he had never heard of, subscribed to magazines he never read, and decided to find out, professionally, why. His method was unusual: for three years he went undercover, answering job adverts and training as a car salesman, a fundraiser, a telemarketer, learning compliance from the people who are paid to produce it. Then he matched what the practitioners did to what the experiments said, and in 1984 published Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion — a book that has sold millions of copies, defined the vocabulary of marketing for forty years, and was written, pointedly, for the person being sold to.

His central discovery is that the tactics all pull the same small set of levers. Human judgement runs on shortcuts: automatic responses to single cues that are usually right, always fast, and completely hijackable by anyone who fakes the cue. Cialdini's image is a tape that clicks on and whirrs to the end. Expensive equals good, so mispriced jewellery sells out at double the price. An expert said so, so the tape plays. Everyone else is doing it, so it must be correct.

The levers, in order. Reciprocation: an uninvited favour creates a debt you never agreed to, repaid on the giver's terms — the free sample, the flower pressed into your hand, the charity's little address labels. Its sneakiest form is rejection-then-retreat: ask for something outrageous, get refused, then concede to what you actually wanted; the concession feels like a gift, and people repay it with a yes. Commitment and consistency: small commitments grow their own justifications. Homeowners who accepted a tiny window sticker about safe driving later agreed, at several times the base rate, to a huge ugly billboard on the lawn. Car dealers lowball, let you build reasons around the decision, then remove the low price — and you buy anyway. Social proof: in uncertainty we decide by headcount. Canned laughter makes weak jokes funnier and everyone knows it and it works anyway; tip jars get salted; and at the dark end of the scale, publicised suicides are followed by copycat spikes, and nine hundred people at Jonestown watched each other's calm and drank. Liking: we say yes to people we like, and liking is manufacturable from attractiveness, similarity, compliments, and association — Tupperware's genius was making the seller your friend, so the request comes from inside your social circle. Authority: we obey symbols, not substance. Nurses in one study prepared an absurd drug order because a voice on the phone said doctor; pedestrians follow a jaywalker in a suit. Scarcity: things become precious as they leave — deadlines, limited editions, cookies from an almost-empty jar rated tastier than identical cookies from a full one. And unity, added in the expanded 2021 edition: the deepest lever of all, we say yes to people we count as us — family, tribe, co-creators, anyone inside the word we.

Every chapter ends the same way: how to say no. That is the book's real distinction. Cialdini frames defence as a public duty — the shortcuts are civilisation's infrastructure, and the right response to counterfeiters is not paranoia but punishment: accept the favour and repay tricks as tricks, name the lever out loud before answering, and in an emergency defeat the bystander freeze by pointing at one person and giving one instruction.

Now the honest ledger, because this book is forty years old and science has moved. The social proof chapter is built on the story of thirty-eight neighbours watching a murder and doing nothing — and a peer-reviewed reconstruction later showed that account false in nearly every particular, though the bystander experiments it inspired are real. The laboratory pillar behind consistency pressure, the induced-compliance paradigm, went through a thirty-nine-lab replication in 2024 and produced nothing. Milgram's obedience work has been reinterpreted. None of this kills the framework — the field tactics are verified daily by the industries that pay for them — but it changes the book's status: a field guide with eroding lab foundations, not settled science. And the 2021 edition, at nearly six hundred pages, doubled the length without auditing the evidence.

So: read the full book if you sell, fundraise, negotiate, or design checkout flows — the tactic detail and defence drills are the working value, and the leaner older paperback is the better read. Stop here if you wanted the seven levers and their reliability report; the framework compresses well because the anecdotes are illustrations, not arguments. Then try it today: find one countdown timer, one free sample, one "only two left," and name the lever before you feel it pull. Once you hear the click, you never quite unhear it.
