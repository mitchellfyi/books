Somewhere over the Pacific, on a long flight, one of the most successful travel writers alive looked out of the window at the moonlit ocean and realised, with sudden embarrassment, that he did not know why the sea is salty. Or how anyone knows what is inside the Earth. Or what an atom actually is, or how old anything was, or why any of it mattered. Bill Bryson had spent his career explaining countries to readers; he could not explain the planet the countries sat on. So he spent the next three years reading, travelling, and pestering patient scientists with elementary questions, and in 2003 he published the result: A Short History of Nearly Everything, five hundred pages that became, and remain, the most successful invitation into science ever written.

The book's secret is its method. It does not really teach science; it tells the story of how the science was won. Six parts carry you from the cosmos to your own species. First the universe: the Big Bang, the birth of the solar system, and the astronomers who slowly discovered how unimaginably large and empty everything is. Then the Earth: the wonderful era when gentleman scientists set out to weigh the planet with mountains and pendulums, and the geologists who stretched its age from thousands of years to billions, scandalising everyone. Then physics remakes reality: Einstein and relativity, the quantum absurdities, the anatomy of the atom, and, in one of the book's darkest detours, the scientist who put lead in petrol and poisoned the world's air for decades. Then the hazards: meteor impacts, earthquakes, and the supervolcano sleeping under Yellowstone, a chapter that lodged permanently in a generation's anxieties. Then life itself, the longest section: cells, Darwin, DNA, the chaos of taxonomy, and extinction's brutal arithmetic, by which nearly every species that has ever existed is gone. And finally us, arriving late, lucky, and precarious.

Everywhere, Bryson's tool is the same: the scale analogy. Textbook diagrams of the solar system are necessary lies, so he rebuilds it to true scale, where planets shrink to peas separated by miles of nothing. The Earth's history compresses into a single day so you can watch humans appear seconds before midnight. Atoms are multiplied through analogies until their smallness finally registers. The book's deepest lesson is scale literacy, an honest feel for how big, how old, and how empty things really are, and no writer has ever delivered it more painlessly.

The cast is the other pleasure. Bryson's history of science is a parade of obsessives, rivals, and magnificently unlucky researchers: the discoverers robbed of credit, the clergymen with fossil collections, the geniuses who were also monsters of pettiness. The famous appear, Newton, Darwin, Einstein, but as flawed humans in contests, never as statues. Science emerges as something people did, with egos and accidents, which is exactly why the book works on readers whom school science bored: it restores the plot.

Now the honest caveat, and with this book it is well documented. Bryson was a confessed amateur synthesising from secondary sources, and specialists promptly catalogued the result: genuine factual errors in the first edition, garbled mechanisms, numbers deployed for wonder rather than accuracy, and a reliance on popular articles as much as on the scientific literature. Later printings fixed some of it; the shape of the criticism stands. The book also cannot help being a snapshot of 2003, and two decades of discovery have moved several of its frontiers. None of this sinks it, because precision was never the cargo. But it defines the proper use: this is a gateway and a wonder-machine, not a reference. Enjoy everything; double-check before you repeat a number at dinner.

The reception told the same double story. It became one of the bestselling science books ever written, won the top science-communication prizes of its era, and colonised school reading lists, while the scientists who admired it kept polite lists of its mistakes. Both responses were correct.

So: read the full book if you want the wonder itself, the entire universe toured in the best company in nonfiction; it reads far shorter than its five hundred pages, and the audiobook suits it. Stop here if you only needed to know what it is and how far to trust it. Skip it if you need citable accuracy or current science; the field books exist for that.

And keep Bryson's founding question, because it is the whole book in miniature. Look at anything today, the sea, the sky, a stone, and ask: do I actually know why that is the way it is? The honest answer started one of the great reading journeys in modern publishing. Yours is available any time.
