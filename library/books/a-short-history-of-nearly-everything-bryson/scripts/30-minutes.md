Somewhere over the Pacific, on a long night flight, one of the most successful travel writers in the world looked out of the window at the moonlit ocean and was ambushed by an embarrassing thought: he did not know why the sea is salty. He pulled the thread and it kept coming. He did not know how anyone could tell what was inside the Earth, or how old the planet was, or what an atom actually looked like, or how you could possibly know what happened in the first moments of the universe. Bill Bryson had spent twenty years explaining countries to readers with more charm than almost anyone alive, and he suddenly realised he could not explain the planet the countries were standing on. Worse, he realised the books that were supposed to explain it had bored him solid at school, written, as he remembered it, as if the authors wanted to keep the good stuff secret, or at least were under instructions never to let the material become too interesting. He suspected a conspiracy of dullness, and he decided to test it. He would spend three years reading everything he could hold, travelling to laboratories, museums, and dig sites, and asking working scientists a long series of blunt, elementary, faintly embarrassing questions, the questions everyone has and nobody asks. Then he would write down the answers in plain English and see whether science, told properly, was actually boring after all.

It was not. A Short History of Nearly Everything, published in 2003, five hundred pages long, became one of the bestselling science books ever written. It won the Aventis Prize, then Britain's top award for science writing, and Bryson gave the winnings to a children's hospital; two years later it won the European Union's Descartes Prize for science communication. It colonised school reading lists, spawned an illustrated edition and a young readers' edition, and for two decades it has been the standard answer to a common and important question: if a person is going to read exactly one book about science in their life, which one should it be? That endorsement, as we will see, comes with a footnote about accuracy that is unusually well documented. But first it is worth understanding what the book actually is, because its title is a small joke with a precise meaning. It is not a history of nearly everything. It is a history of how we found out about nearly everything, and that difference is the entire design.

Bryson does not really teach science. He tells the story of how the science was won. You do not get a lecture on radiometric dating; you get the century-long, frequently farcical struggle to put a number on the Earth's age, complete with ruined careers and stolen credit. You do not get the periodic table; you get the strange, secretive men who assembled it element by element. An academic reviewer put his finger on exactly this point: the book's true subject is not knowledge but the history of knowing, and every fact arrives attached to the person who fought for it, usually a person you have never heard of, frequently a person history treated shabbily. The approach has a real cost, and any honest account owes you the price before the end. But it solves the exact problem Bryson started with. School science bores people because it hands over conclusions with the plot removed, and the plot, it turns out, is sensational: rivalry, obsession, luck, fraud, decades of patient grinding, and an amount of personal eccentricity that no novelist would dare invent.

The tour runs in six parts, from the largest possible scale down to the fact of you. Part one, Lost in the Cosmos, opens with the hardest question available, how a universe begins, and narrates it as the biography of an idea. The Big Bang arrives not as an equation but as a contested, resisted, gradually victorious inference, named, with maximum dismissiveness, by a scientist who never believed in it. The clinching evidence is one of the book's best stories: two radio engineers in New Jersey plagued by a persistent hiss in a giant antenna, who cleaned out the nesting pigeons and scrubbed the droppings and still could not kill the noise, and who turned out to be listening to the leftover radiation of creation itself, the faint static of the universe's first light, arriving from every direction at once. They had found the most important sound in cosmology while trying to get rid of it. It is the book's method in miniature: the discovery matters, but the story of the discovery is what makes it stick.

The same section establishes the book's favourite kind of hero. The man who showed that the universe is expanding, and vastly larger than anyone had believed, was a preening, self-mythologising athlete of an astronomer; the assistant whose painstaking measurements underwrote the discovery had started professional life driving mule trains up the mountain to build the observatory, and taught himself astronomy on the job. And out at the amateur end of the telescope, Bryson finds a country clergyman with a modest back-garden instrument and a freakish gift: he holds the field of view of thousands of galaxies in his memory, so that when a single new point of light appears, a star detonating in a galaxy of billions, he notices. Professionals with computers struggle to match him. The universe is unimaginably vast, and one hobbyist's trained attention still counts in it: that juxtaposition, wonder plus human oddity, is the engine the whole book runs on.

Then the solar system assembles, and Bryson delivers the move that made the book famous, the honest scale model. Every classroom diagram of the solar system, he points out, is a necessary lie; to fit the planets on one page you must throw away the distances, which are the whole point. Rebuild it honestly, with the Earth shrunk to something like a pea, and Jupiter sits hundreds of metres away, Pluto a mile or more distant and smaller than a bacterium at that scale, and the nearest star is not on the diagram, or in the same country as the diagram. The overwhelming truth about space, the one the posters hide, is its emptiness. This is the first of the book's three great scale corrections, and they are its deepest educational gift: not facts but a recalibrated intuition, an honest feel for how big, how old, and how small things actually are.

Part two, The Size of the Earth, is the book's happiest stretch, the golden age of gentleman science, when the planet was measured by men in waistcoats with private incomes and unlimited patience. Here are the surveyors dragging measuring chains across fevered jungles and up mountains to establish the length of a degree; here are the clergymen-naturalists assembling fossil collections between sermons; and here, magnificently, is Henry Cavendish, quite possibly the finest experimental scientist of his age and so pathologically shy that his housekeeper communicated with him by letter, who in a shed, with lead weights and a torsion wire, effectively weighed the Earth. Here too are the fossil wars, the era when the bones of vanished monsters turned respectable science into a blood feud: rival collectors bribing each other's diggers, racing each other into print, naming species out of spite and sometimes assembling them wrong in the rush, a reminder that palaeontology was built as much on hatred as on patience. And here is geology's great scandal, the age of the world. The confident Biblical arithmetic that dated creation to a few thousand years ago, calculated from the begats with a straight face and defended with a straighter one, collides with rock that will not cooperate, with strata and fossils and erosion rates that demand spans of time so vast they frightened the men who first glimpsed them. Even the greatest physicist of the Victorian age weighed in with authoritative underestimates, calculated from how fast a molten Earth should cool, and was wrong for a reason nobody yet knew: the planet has its own heating system, radioactivity, warming it from within and resetting every clock built without it. The number stretches, fought over at every step, from thousands of years to millions to billions, and the fight produces a lovely irony that Bryson savours: the man who finally nailed the true age of the Earth, in the middle of the twentieth century, did it by measuring lead in ancient meteorite fragments, and then spent the rest of his career in a furious, lonely, ultimately successful campaign against the lead industry itself, whose product was quietly poisoning the air of every city on the planet. Dating the world and defending it turned out to be the same skill set.

Deep time is the second great scale correction, and Bryson compresses it the only way that works, by analogy. Squeeze the Earth's whole history into a single day and life appears surprisingly early, but stays microscopic for most of the day; nothing you could see without a lens until deep into the evening; the dinosaurs arrive late at night and are gone before the day ends; humans appear in roughly the final minute, and all of recorded history occupies the last instants before midnight, the thickness of a coat of paint. Nobody who absorbs that image ever hears the phrase "millions of years" the same way again, which is precisely the point.

Part three, A New Age Dawns, hands the story to physics, and the pace turns vertiginous. It opens with the third scale correction, downward this time: the atom. Atoms are so small that no everyday analogy survives contact with them, so Bryson stacks analogies until the smallness registers, and then adds the detail every reader remembers: atoms are effectively eternal and endlessly recycled, so the material of you has been many things before, and statistically some of it has passed through other human beings on its way to becoming you. You are, among other things, a secondhand shop of the dead. The atom's own interior compounds the vertigo: it is almost entirely empty space, its mass concentrated in a nucleus so proportionally tiny that the solidity of the world, the table your hand will not pass through, is revealed as an electrical negotiation rather than a fact of matter. From there the twentieth century detonates. Einstein, working in a patent office, rebuilds space and time into a single flexible fabric; the quantum physicists then dismantle the certainty underneath even that, establishing to general dismay, including Einstein's, that at the smallest scales nature deals in probabilities and refuses to be watched without changing its behaviour. Bryson is disarmingly honest about the limits of paraphrase here. Nobody, he cheerfully reports, can truly claim to understand the quantum world; the physicists themselves say as much; the sane response is careful awe, and he supplies it.

The same section carries the book's darkest biography, and it is dark enough that Bryson's comic timing goes quiet. Thomas Midgley was an American engineer of genuine brilliance who solved two industrial problems in a row. He solved engine knock by putting lead into petrol, and he solved refrigeration's toxic-gas problem by inventing chlorofluorocarbons, which ate the ozone layer. One competent, well-meaning, commercially incentivised man thereby did more damage to the atmosphere than any other single organism in history, and the lead alone quietly raised the body burden of an entire century of city dwellers. It is the book's standing counterweight to its own celebration: the ingenuity that decodes the atom is the same ingenuity that, unsupervised and profitably wrong, can poison the world, and the scientist who finally forced lead out of petrol had to fight the industry for decades to do it.

Part four, Dangerous Planet, is the inventory of hazards, and it is the section that lodged permanently in a generation's imagination. It opens with impacts: the slow realisation, resisted like every inconvenient idea in this book, that the Earth sits in a shooting gallery, that craters are not ancient volcanoes but wounds, and that the dinosaurs' disappearance has an address in the Yucatán. The impact hypothesis itself gets the full Bryson treatment, because it is a perfect specimen of how science actually receives a correct idea: a father-and-son team, a physicist and a geologist, propose that a thin worldwide layer of a rare element marks the day a mountain-sized rock ended an era, and the geological establishment spends years treating them as vulgar catastrophists, before the crater itself, vast and buried and hiding in plain sight in survey data, settles the argument. Being right, in this book, is rarely the same thing as being believed, and the gap between the two is where all the best stories live. The statistics of impact are queasy precisely because they are neither negligible nor imminent, rare on any human timescale and certain on a planetary one. Earthquakes follow, shrugging off cities along faults we map but cannot time. And under Wyoming sits the book's signature anxiety, the one readers reliably quote at dinner parties: Yellowstone is not a national park with a volcano in it; the park, in effect, is the volcano, a caldera roughly the size of the park itself, fed by a plume of molten rock, with a history of eruptions on a cycle measured in hundreds of thousands of years and a most recent entry old enough to make the arithmetic uncomfortable. Bryson's hazard chapters are the book at its most cinematic, and, fair warning, they are also where specialists have most often quibbled with his framing of frequencies and odds. The underlying lesson survives the quibbles comfortably: the planet's calm is a temporary local condition, not a promise, and civilisation has flourished inside a quiet interval it did nothing to earn.

Part five, Life Itself, is the longest section and the deepest, and it opens by narrowing the stage. Before life can be interesting it has to be possible, and the band of possibility is thin: the right distance from the right kind of star, a moon large enough to steady the planet's wobble, an atmosphere just thick enough, oceans, plate tectonics recycling the crust, and a magnetic field holding the solar wind off. The ocean chapters alone justify the section: the sea is most of the habitable volume of the planet, and we have explored almost none of it; the deep floor is less mapped than the Moon. Then life itself, and Bryson refuses to normalise it. Getting from chemistry to a self-copying cell is so improbable that its single known success has to carry the entire story, and yet once life starts it proves nearly impossible to stop, surviving frozen oceans, poisoned skies, and rock-melting bombardment across billions of years, mostly, it must be said, as slime. Complexity is a late luxury; for the majority of life's history, visiting Earth would have meant admiring bacteria. And the age of bacteria, Bryson is careful to note, never actually ended: microbes still own the planet by any fair census, outnumbering and outweighing the visible world, running the chemistry that keeps it habitable, living in boiling springs and bedrock and, in their trillions, in you, mostly unnamed and entirely indifferent to the newcomers taking credit for the place.

The human cast of this section is the book's warmest. Darwin, brilliant and terrified, sits on the most important idea in biology for twenty years because he can see exactly what it will cost him, and is finally stampeded into publishing by a letter from a younger man who has had the same idea in a malarial fever. The structure of DNA falls to an odd, self-assembled team in Cambridge, in a story tangled with credit that was distributed with the era's usual unfairness, most famously at the expense of the woman whose X-ray images made the discovery possible. Taxonomy arrives as glorious chaos: a Swedish botanist with a filthy imagination and a genius for filing invents the naming system, and two and a half centuries later science still cannot say, to the nearest few tens of millions, how many species share the planet with us. The described species are a rounding error on the estimates; the catalogue of life is barely begun.

And through the whole section runs the arithmetic of extinction, the book's most quietly radical statistic: something over ninety-nine per cent of every species that has ever existed is gone. Extinction is not the exception in life's story; it is the rule, the default fate, the thing that happens to essentially everyone. The dinosaurs, who read as failures because they ended, ran the planet for well over a hundred million years; we have managed a small fraction of one per cent of that. Every living thing you have ever seen is the current end of an unbroken chain of survivors, and the chain has passed through bottlenecks that nearly cut it. Bryson lets that fact do its own work. It recasts existence, yours included, as a lottery win of preposterous odds, and it sets up the book's ending better than any argument could.

Part six, The Road to Us, brings the story home, first through ice, because human beings turn out to be creatures of the ice ages, our whole run as a species conducted inside a flickering climate interval, and then through bones. The hominid fossil record, Bryson notes with visible delight, is so sparse that the crucial evidence for millions of years of human ancestry could fit in the back of a van, and the field built on it is correspondingly rich in ego, feud, and the occasional outright fraud; rarely has so much confident theory rested on so few kilograms of material. Out of that record comes the story's final improbability: multiple human species once shared the planet, and all of them ended except one lineage, ours, which squeaked through its own near-misses with no guarantee attached. We arrived a moment ago, geologically speaking, lucky beyond calculation, and the book closes on the moral it has been assembling for five hundred pages. We are the one species that got to find any of this out, the universe's way, so far as we know, of knowing itself, and in the brief interval since we arrived we have been a careless custodian of the only planet confirmed to permit any of it. It is an existence, Bryson says in effect, that deserves better attention than we usually pay it, and his book is a five-hundred-page demonstration of how to pay it.

Across all six parts, the cast is the constant pleasure, and the casting policy is deliberate. Bryson's history of science is a parade of the overlooked: the assistants who did the work while the professor took the credit, the rivals who lost naming rights by a week, the mule driver who became a great astronomer, the clergymen with world-class fossil collections, the amateur in an armchair who out-thought the professionals, the supernova hunter scanning the sky from a back garden. The famous appear, Newton, Darwin, Einstein, but as flawed humans in contests, never as marble: Newton the incomparable genius who also poured years into alchemy and Biblical numerology and once pushed a needle into his own eye socket out of curiosity; Darwin the revolutionary too anxious to publish. Science emerges as something people did, with egos and accidents and mortgages, and this is exactly why the book works on readers whom school failed: it restores the plot, and the plot has characters. But the policy is also a mild distortion, and it should be said plainly. Great-oddball history flattens the institutional reality of science, the funding committees, the collaborations, the unglamorous replication, behind personalities, and it teaches the drama of discovery more reliably than the content discovered. Readers finish the book knowing how we found out, and glowing about it, while the science itself, the actual mechanism of the greenhouse effect or radioactive decay, often remains at postcard depth. The book's method is its charm, and the charm has a syllabus-shaped hole.

Which brings us to the accuracy question, and with this book it is not a vague grumble, it is a documented record. Bryson was a confessed amateur synthesising from secondary sources, popular science books, magazine articles, and interviews, as much as from the scientific literature, and the professionals promptly did what professionals do. One science website published a formal catalogue of errors in the first edition, genuine slips of number, unit, and mechanism, alongside, it should be said, a recommendation of the book. A physics-community review praised the sweep while noting the wobbles. Later printings corrected part of the list; early printings and derived editions carry it intact. None of this is fatal, because precision was never the cargo this book was carrying; wonder was, and wonder survives an erratum. But it defines the terms of use precisely. A Short History is a gateway and a wonder-machine, not a reference. Its shape is trustworthy, its stories are broadly sound, and its individual numbers should be checked before you repeat them, which, given how quotable the book is, is a warning its readers genuinely need.

The reception embodied that double verdict without ever resolving it. On one side: colossal sales, translation into dozens of languages, the Aventis and Descartes prizes, a place on school syllabuses, and twenty years of readers, including a measurable population of working scientists, who trace their interest in science to this one book. On the other: reviewers with lists, polite, specific, published lists of its mistakes. Both responses were correct, and the book's status today is exactly their sum: the best-loved on-ramp to science in modern publishing, cited with a footnote.

On content alone, the book earns five point two out of ten, and the reasons are instructive rather than damning. Explanatory power is real but shallow by design: the scale analogies and discovery narratives genuinely recalibrate a reader's intuitions, but the science underneath is compressed to postcards, and some postcards are smudged. Evidence is the weak flank: secondhand sourcing with a specialist-catalogued error rate is a real cost in a book that most readers treat as authoritative. Insight value lands mainly as reorientation, scale literacy, deep time, existence as statistical fluke, which for a general reader can be permanent furniture, and for a scientifically literate one is familiar. Practical utility is nearly nil, by mutual agreement between author and reader; nothing here applies to Monday morning. Completeness is generous in breadth, honest about ignorance, and inevitably a snapshot of 2003, thin on fields that have since exploded. What rescues the score is efficiency: for five hundred pages spanning all of science, remarkably few are wasted, the subjects rotate before any can outstay its welcome, and the prose never charges admission twice for the same idea.

So, the decision. Read the full book if what you want is the wonder itself, because that is the one thing no summary can carry: the grand tour in the best company in nonfiction, five hundred pages that read far shorter, ideally in a recent corrected edition, and the audiobook suits its anecdotes admirably. It remains close to the perfect gift for a curious teenager, or for any adult whose science education failed them and who suspects, correctly, that it should not have. The number of scientists who trace their start to it is the best review it will ever get. Stop here if you needed the book's shape, its method, its greatest stories, and an exact calibration of how far to trust it; you now have all four. Skip the book entirely if you need accuracy you can cite, depth in a single field, or science more current than 2003; two decades of discovery have moved several of its frontiers, from planets around other stars to the human family tree, and a modern field-specific book will serve you better. Bryson, the least defensive of authors, would probably hand you one himself.

Three things are worth carrying away permanently. First, the method, which is portable: learn any subject as the history of how we know, because facts stick when the discovery story comes attached, and the trick works far beyond science. Second, the scales: keep an honest feel for how big, how old, how empty, and how small, and distrust any diagram that makes the universe look cosy; nearly everything misleading ever said about nature begins with a picture drawn to fit the page. Third, the arithmetic of your own existence: nearly everything that ever lived is extinct, the chain that produced you never once broke across billions of years, and the reasonable response to that is not anxiety but attention.

And keep Bryson's founding question, because it is the whole book in miniature and it costs nothing to use. Pick anything ordinary today, the sea, the sky, a stone in the road, the salt on the table, and ask yourself honestly: do I actually know why that is the way it is, or do I just know the words for it? Asked once, over the Pacific, by a travel writer with no scientific training whatsoever, that question produced the most successful invitation into science ever written. It is still the best place to start, and unlike the universe it describes, it is fully within reach.
