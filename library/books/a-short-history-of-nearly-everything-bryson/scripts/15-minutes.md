Somewhere over the Pacific, on a long night flight, one of the most successful travel writers in the world looked out of the window at the moonlit ocean and was ambushed by an embarrassing thought: he did not know why the sea is salty. He did not know how anyone knew what was inside the Earth, or how old the planet was, or what an atom actually looked like, or how a universe gets started in the first place. Bill Bryson had spent twenty years explaining countries to readers, and he suddenly realised he could not explain the planet the countries were standing on. Worse, he realised that the books that were supposed to explain it had bored him solid at school, as if the authors were keeping the good parts secret. So he did the most Bryson thing imaginable: he spent the next three years reading everything, travelling to laboratories and dig sites, and asking patient scientists a long series of blunt, elementary questions. In 2003 he published the result. A Short History of Nearly Everything became one of the bestselling science books ever written, won the era's top prizes for science writing, and for two decades has been the default answer to a common question: if someone reads exactly one book about science, which should it be?

Here is the trick that makes it work, and it is worth stating precisely, because it is the whole design. The book does not really teach science. It tells the story of how the science was won. You do not get a lecture on radiometric dating; you get the decades-long, frequently farcical struggle to put a number on the Earth's age, complete with careers ruined and credit stolen. An academic reviewer put his finger on exactly this: Bryson's subject is not knowledge but the history of knowing, every fact arriving attached to the person who fought for it. That approach has a genuine cost, which we will come to, but it solves the problem Bryson started with. School science bores people because it hands over conclusions with the plot removed. Bryson puts the plot back, and it turns out the plot is sensational: rivalry, obsession, luck, decades of patient grinding, and an astonishing amount of eccentricity.

The tour runs in six parts, from the largest scale to the most personal. Part one, Lost in the Cosmos, opens with the Big Bang, narrated not as physics but as biography of an idea: how anyone could possibly claim to know what happened in the universe's first fractions of a second, and why the evidence, faint radiation still humming through the sky, convinced the doubters. Then the solar system assembles, and Bryson delivers the move that made the book famous: the honest scale model. Textbook diagrams of the solar system, he points out, are necessary lies; on a page, the planets have to be crammed together or you could not print them. Drawn to true scale, with the Earth reduced to a pea, Jupiter sits hundreds of metres away and Pluto a mile and a half distant, and the overwhelming lesson of space becomes its emptiness. Nobody who absorbs that paragraph looks at a classroom poster the same way again.

Part two, The Size of the Earth, is the book's happiest stretch, the golden age of gentleman science. Here are the surveyors dragging chains through fever-ridden jungles to measure a single degree of longitude, the Reverend clergymen with fossil collections, and the magnificent Henry Cavendish, so pathologically shy that his housekeeper communicated with him by letter, weighing the entire planet with lead balls and a torsion wire in a shed. Here too is geology's scandal: the slow, evidence-forced stretching of the Earth's age from the Bible's few thousand years to something so vast it broke the imagination of the people who discovered it. Deep time is the second great scale lesson, and Bryson compresses it the only way that works, into analogy: squeeze Earth's history into a single day and life appears by breakfast, but nothing bigger than a microbe until the evening; humans arrive seconds before midnight. All of recorded history is the thickness of the final flake of paint.

Part three, A New Age Dawns, hands the story to physics, and the pace turns vertiginous. It opens with the third great scale shock, downward this time: the atom. Atoms are so small that the number of them in a single breath outnumbers anything the imagination can grip, so Bryson does what he always does and multiplies analogies until the smallness finally registers, then adds the detail everyone remembers, that atoms are so durable and so promiscuous that every one of us carries atoms that once passed through other people entirely. From there, Einstein rebuilds space and time; the quantum physicists then dismantle the certainty underneath even that, to Einstein's lasting irritation. Bryson is honest about the limits of paraphrase here: nobody, he cheerfully reports, truly understands the quantum world; the sane response is careful awe. The section also contains the book's darkest and most memorable biography: Thomas Midgley, the American engineer who put lead into petrol and then, for an encore, invented the chlorofluorocarbons that ate the ozone layer, a one-man environmental catastrophe whose inventions poisoned the air for most of a century. It is Bryson's quiet counterweight to the celebration: the same ingenuity that decodes the atom can, with commercial incentives and no oversight, do planetary harm.

Part four, Dangerous Planet, is the inventory of hazards, and it lodged in a generation's imagination. Meteor impacts arrive with the queasy statistics of things that are rare but not rare enough. Earthquakes shrug off cities. And under Wyoming sits the book's signature anxiety: Yellowstone, revealed to be not a park with a volcano in it but the caldera of a supervolcano the size of the park itself, on an eruption cycle measured in hundreds of thousands of years. Bryson's hazard chapters are the book at its most dramatic, and, fair warning, the place where specialists have most often quibbled with his framing of frequencies and risk. The underlying point survives the quibbles: the planet's calm is a temporary local condition, not a promise.

Part five, Life Itself, is the longest section and the deepest. The cell, the improbable chemistry of getting life started at all, Darwin sitting on his theory for twenty years, the double helix, and the gloriously chaotic business of taxonomy, where science still cannot say, to the nearest few million, how many species share the planet. Two themes dominate. The first is extinction's arithmetic: nearly every species that has ever existed, well over ninety-nine percent, is gone, which recasts every living thing you have ever seen as a lottery winner. The second is ignorance, honestly mapped. We have described perhaps a few percent of the species that exist; the deep sea is less charted than the Moon; the book keeps a running, humbling tally of how much of the map is still blank. Bryson treats that ignorance as the invitation it is. Two decades on, some of those frontiers have moved, exoplanets, human origins, the microbiome, which is worth remembering: the book is a snapshot of 2003, not a live feed.

Part six, The Road to Us, brings the story home through the bone-hunters and the hominid fossils, a field so starved of evidence, Bryson notes, that you could fit the crucial specimens in the back of a van, and so rich in ego that the fights over them fill libraries. Humans arrive at the very end, late, lucky, and precarious, and the book closes on the moral it has been building all along: we are the one species that got to find any of this out, which is a staggering privilege, and we have been a remarkably careless custodian of the planet that permitted it.

Across all six parts, the cast is the constant pleasure. Bryson's history of science is deliberately a parade of the overlooked: the assistants who did the work while the professor took the credit, the rivals who lost naming rights by a week, the amateurs and clergymen and one-obsession cranks who assembled modern knowledge almost by accident. The famous appear, Newton, Darwin, Einstein, but as flawed humans in contests, never as marble. Newton is a genius who also spent years on alchemy and Biblical numerology; Darwin is brilliant and terrified. This cast policy is charming, and it is also a mild distortion, because it flattens the institutional reality of science, the funding, the collaboration, the unglamorous replication, behind personalities. The book teaches science as an adventure of individuals, which is partly why it is loved and partly why it is not quite true.

Which brings us to the accuracy question, and with this book it is not a vague grumble; it is documented. Bryson was a confessed amateur synthesising from secondary sources, popular science books and articles as much as the scientific literature, and specialists promptly did what specialists do. One science site published a formal catalogue of errors in the first edition, genuine slips of number and mechanism, alongside its recommendation of the book. Reviewers in the physics community praised the sweep while noting the wobbles. Later printings corrected some of the list; early printings and the audiobook carry it intact. None of this is fatal, because precision was never the cargo the book was carrying; wonder was. But it does define the proper use. A Short History is a gateway and a wonder-machine, not a reference. Enjoy all of it; check any specific number before you repeat it at dinner. The reception embodied that double verdict perfectly: bestseller lists and school reading lists on one side, the era's top science-communication prizes, and, on the other, scientists who admired it while keeping polite lists of its mistakes. Both responses were correct, and neither cancels the other.

Judged on what it teaches and how it reads, the book earns five point nine out of ten, and the reasons are instructive rather than damning. Explanatory power is real but postcard-deep; you finish knowing the stories of discovery better than the science discovered. Evidence is the weak flank: secondhand sourcing with a catalogued error rate is a real cost even in a book this warm. Insight lands mainly as reorientation, scale literacy, deep time, existence as statistical fluke, which for a general reader can be genuinely permanent, and for a scientifically literate one is familiar. Practical utility is nearly nil, by design; nothing here applies to Monday. What lifts the book is the writing itself, with efficiency and honesty close behind: prose this charming is craft of the rarest kind and is credited as such, remarkably few of five hundred pages are wasted, the subjects rotate before any can outstay its welcome, and it never pretends the map is finished. The content is a superb invitation rather than a substantial meal — but the invitation is engraved, and that is worth real credit.

The decision, then. Read the full book if what you want is the wonder itself: the grand tour in the best company in nonfiction, five hundred pages that read far shorter, ideally in a recent corrected edition; the audiobook suits its anecdotes. It is close to the perfect gift for a curious teenager or for any adult whose science education failed them, and the number of working scientists who trace their start to this book is its best review. Stop at a summary if you only needed to know what the book does, its shape, and exactly how far to trust it; you now do. Skip it entirely if you need accuracy you can cite, depth in a single field, or science more current than 2003; a modern field-specific book will serve you better, and Bryson himself would probably hand you one.

Three things are worth carrying out of it. First, the method: learn science as the history of how we know, because facts stick when the discovery story comes with them, and you can apply that trick to any subject you are trying to learn. Second, the scales: keep an honest feel for how big, how old, and how empty things are, and distrust any diagram that makes the universe look cosy. Third, Bryson's founding question, which is the whole book in miniature and free to use. Pick anything ordinary today, the sea, the sky, a stone in the road, and ask: do I actually know why that is the way it is, or do I just know the words? The honest answer to that question, asked on a plane over the Pacific, produced one of the great reading journeys in modern publishing. It is still the best place to start.
