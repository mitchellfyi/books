A broken statue gives an order. Rilke stood in front of an ancient torso of Apollo — no head, no arms, no eyes — and ended his poem about it with a line that has bothered readers ever since: you must change your life. There is no face to issue the command and no authority behind it, yet the demand lands anyway. Peter Sloterdijk, a German philosopher who ran an art school in Karlsruhe for fourteen years and enjoys causing trouble, takes that line as the pure form of every imperative to improve, and builds five hundred pages on it.

His central claim is deliberately outrageous: religions do not exist. What exist are exercise systems. Look at what the traditions actually consist of, he says, and you find not doctrines but drills — fasting, prayer at fixed hours, repetition, submission to a rule, supervision by a teacher who has done it longer than you. Doctrine is what practitioners say about the practice afterwards. On this reading, monastic orders, ancient philosophical schools, yoga, military training and modern self-optimisation belong to a single family. The human being, constitutionally unfinished, is the animal that trains. He calls the techniques anthropotechnics, and the whole of history becomes the record of them.

That yields his sharpest tool: vertical tension. What makes anyone submit to difficulty is a perceived gap between where they are and a more accomplished state they can sense but cannot yet reach. Cultures organise themselves around such heights. The emblem is the acrobat, suspended between the merely human and something above it. His most memorable case is Carl Hermann Unthan, born without arms in the nineteenth century, who learned to play the violin with his feet and built an entire life out of converting an impossibility into a routine. Sloterdijk's point is not inspiration. It is that this is what everyone is doing, less visibly.

Then the historical argument. Modernity, he says, did not abolish asceticism; it nationalised it. The demand to change your life became a demand to change life in general, and training moved out of the monastery into the school, the gymnasium, the clinic, the factory and the state's programmes for improving whole populations. His clean example is Pierre de Coubertin reviving the Olympic Games as an explicitly modern, secular institution built on something very like cultic devotion to training — and, at the other end, L. Ron Hubbard assembling a movement out of graded exercises. Exercise systems flourish exactly where doctrine has evaporated.

Now the honest ledger, because this book has real problems. It proves nothing. The method is erudite association across Rilke, Nietzsche, Kafka, Cioran, Wittgenstein and Bourdieu, and there is no test the thesis could fail — define religion as practice and almost any repeated activity qualifies, which is why even admirers describe the theme as a catch-all. The critic Nina Power put the political objection most sharply: the self at the centre of the book is assumed rather than examined, the ideal being celebrated belongs to an educated class with time to contemplate, and the practical upshot amounts to pulling your socks up while saying nothing about paid work, money or caring for other people. She also notes that women are almost entirely absent from a five-hundred-page book about how human beings form themselves — Simone Weil and Hannah Arendt get passing mentions. And he treats dullness and dejection as more pressing human problems than exploitation and war.

The prose divides people. One specialist praises the pleasure of the text, the vigour of the writing, the novelty of the language. A less patient reviewer calls it stodgy and long-winded, and suspects the sheer volume defeated the translator in places. Both are describing the same book. It rates five point four out of ten — limited — strong on insight and on explanatory reach, weak on evidence, on completeness, and especially on efficiency: one idea restated for five hundred pages.

So what should you do? If you read philosophy for pleasure, read it — the Rilke, Nietzsche and Kafka chapters are genuinely fine, and the sweep is the point. Everyone else: take the idea, which is portable and does not need the apparatus. Watch what you actually rehearse rather than what you believe, because the repetitions are what shape you. Notice the height you are looking up at, because that is what makes the difficulty attractive. And ask who set the regime you are following — schools, employers and governments all issue training programmes, and Sloterdijk's real gift is making you see them as programmes. If you want a method rather than a lens, this is the wrong book entirely.
