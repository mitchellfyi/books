Some time around 1908, Rainer Maria Rilke stood in a Paris museum in front of a piece of broken marble. It was an archaic torso of Apollo: no head, no arms, no eyes, just the trunk of a god carved by someone whose name nobody knows. Rilke wrote a short poem about it, and the poem does something strange at the end. Having described this mutilated stone, he says that there is no place that does not see you. And then, with no preparation and no argument, the last line arrives: you must change your life.

Peter Sloterdijk has written five hundred pages about that sentence, and the thing that grips him is its structure rather than its sentiment. Here is an absolute imperative — not a suggestion, not encouragement, a command — and there is nobody issuing it. No god speaks. No priest interprets. No law compels. A headless piece of rock in a museum somehow makes a demand on a man standing in front of it, and the demand is felt as binding. Sloterdijk's wager is that this is the pure form of every order to improve that human beings have ever obeyed. Underneath the theology, the ideology, the therapy and the motivational packaging, what remains is this: a felt command to become something you are not yet, arriving from a source you cannot locate.

Before going further, it is worth knowing who is talking, because this book is inseparable from its author's habits of mind. Peter Sloterdijk was born in Karlsruhe in 1947 to a German mother and a Dutch father. He studied philosophy, history and German at Munich and Hamburg between 1968 and 1974, and finished a doctorate on autobiography in the Weimar Republic. Between 1978 and 1980 he lived at Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh's ashram in Pune — which is to say that this theorist of spiritual exercise systems spent two years inside one. In 1983 he published Critique of Cynical Reason, which became one of the best-selling philosophy books in post-war Germany. He became a professor at the Karlsruhe University of Arts and Design in 1992 and was its rector from 2001 to 2015, and for a decade he co-hosted a philosophy discussion programme on German television. He is a public intellectual of a kind that barely exists in English: enormously well read, deliberately provocative, and entirely comfortable causing a national argument.

His provocation here is compact. Religions do not exist.

He does not mean that nobody is religious. He means that religion is the wrong unit of analysis, and that using it has made a whole debate unintelligible. Look closely at what any religious tradition consists of, moment by moment, and you will not find a set of propositions that members affirm. You will find drills. Fasting. Prayer at fixed hours — five times daily, or seven, or at every change of the watch. Repetition. Silence. Postures held. Texts recited until they are known by heart. Submission to a rule, a novitiate, a period of testing, and a teacher who has done this for longer than you and who corrects your form when it slips. The doctrines, Sloterdijk argues, are not the substance of the thing. Doctrine is what practitioners produce afterwards, when an outsider asks them to explain what they are doing and why.

Once you accept that inversion, the familiar categories fall apart in an interesting way. A Benedictine monastery, a Stoic school in Athens, a yoga lineage, a Zen hall, a military academy, a conservatoire and a modern fitness regime stop looking like fundamentally different institutions. They are all exercise systems: structured programmes for working on human material until it changes shape. And the creature at the centre of all of them is, in Sloterdijk's phrase, constitutionally unfinished. We are not the rational animal or the political animal. We are the animal that has to train.

He gives the techniques a name — anthropotechnics, the technologies by which humans form themselves, individually and collectively — and then rereads several thousand years of history as the record of them. That is the book. Its title is the command, and its subject is everyone who has ever tried to obey one.

The immediate payoff is a demolition of a debate that Europe and America have been having for thirty years about whether religion is returning. Sloterdijk's answer is that the question is malformed. Nothing is returning, because nothing left. What happened at secularisation is that the exercise systems were redistributed. The drills carried on under new management and new names, and the fact that they no longer come with gods attached does not make them any less what they always were.

That leads him to the single most useful idea in the book, and the one most worth taking even if you never open it. He calls it vertical tension.

Start with the obvious question: why would anybody volunteer for difficulty? Training is unpleasant. Nobody enjoys the fourth hour of scales, or the third week of a fast, or the part of the marathon where the interesting scenery has run out. The usual answers involve willpower, discipline, character — qualities some people are supposed to have more of. Sloterdijk finds this uninformative. His answer is that practice begins the moment a gap becomes perceptible: a difference between where you currently stand and a more accomplished state that you can somehow sense above you and cannot yet reach.

Cultures, he argues, are organised around such heights. They install figures at the top — the saint, the sage, the master, the virtuoso, the champion — and the effect is not moral pressure but something closer to gravity working upwards. You do not decide to be pulled. You notice a height and the pull is already operating. His emblem for the person caught in that field is the acrobat: the figure suspended between the merely human and something above it, held there by nothing except continuous practice. He goes as far as proposing an acrobatic ethics, a way of living organised around the conquest of the improbable.

His most vivid case is a man named Carl Hermann Unthan, born in the nineteenth century without arms, who learned to play the violin with his feet, and to shoot, and to write, and who spent a career on stage doing it. Sloterdijk is emphatically not telling this story to inspire anyone. He is using Unthan as the clearest available specimen of what he thinks all of us are doing all the time, only less visibly: taking something that ought to be impossible, and grinding it down through repetition into a routine that looks, from outside, like a natural ability.

Nietzsche stands behind much of this, and Sloterdijk is honest about the debt. He reads Nietzsche as having glimpsed an ascetic planet — an antiquity organised around self-overcoming rather than belief — and treats his own book as finishing a project Nietzsche started and abandoned. Where Nietzsche saw asceticism as largely a sickness, a turning of strength against itself, Sloterdijk wants to recover it as a technology, morally neutral in itself, that can be turned to any end at all.

If practice needs a height, it also needs somebody standing at it, and a substantial part of the book concerns the trainer. The master, the coach, the abbot, the guru, the teacher: Sloterdijk calls this territory master games. The trainer's function, he argues, is not primarily to transmit content. It is to act as living proof that the exaggeration is possible — to have been further up the slope than you have, and to keep insisting that the next impossible thing is merely difficult. That is why every exercise system generates hierarchy, why the relationship with a teacher is so charged, and why leaving one feels less like changing your mind than like apostasy.

It also gives him an elegant, slightly deflating account of political upheaval. A great deal of what gets called revolution, he suggests, is in fact a change of trainer. The demand to practise survives intact; what changes is the authority that sets the drills and defines the standard. The population goes on being trained. It simply reports to somebody else.

There is a darker current running through all this, and the book is much better for it. Kafka's hunger artists appear — the performers who starve themselves in public until audiences lose interest and drift away — as the reductio of the practising life: discipline refined to the point where it consumes its practitioner and serves nobody at all. The Romanian pessimist Cioran, whom Sloterdijk reads as a sort of Parisian Buddhist, supplies a different failure mode: exercise as an elegant means of withdrawing from life rather than intensifying it. These chapters matter because they stop the argument being a celebration. Practice can hollow a person out. Exercises can go wrong. A large amount of what people submit themselves to is, in his term, misexercise — drill that produces nothing but the capacity to keep drilling.

That thread arrives at one of his finest formulations: culture itself operates as a monastic rule. Every society issues a schedule. When you rise. What you repeat. What counts as slacking. What a wasted day looks like. It enforces this without ever writing it down as a rule and without anyone experiencing it as discipline, because it arrived in the guise of normality. The monastery did not disappear; it expanded until we stopped being able to see its walls.

It is worth knowing how the book is actually laid out, because the shape explains a good deal about the experience of reading it. After an introduction announcing the anthropotechnic turn, the first part is called The Planet of the Practising, and it is essentially a gallery: Rilke's commanding statue, Nietzsche's ascetic antiquity, Unthan, Kafka, Cioran, and then the chapter that denies religions exist and puts Coubertin and Hubbard side by side. The second part attempts a history of the practising life proper — its programmes, its withdrawals, its masters, its revolutions. The third, titled Exaggeration Procedures, follows the ascetic impulse into secular modernity: the withdrawn subject returning to the world, art practised upon human beings, exercises that fail, and culture as monastic rule. A closing outlook turns the whole thing towards the present and the planetary problem.

Two ideas from the middle of that structure deserve rescuing, because they rarely get quoted and they are among the better things in the book. The first is what he calls the first eccentricity: the founding move of the practising life is a secession. To begin practising seriously is to step out of ordinary existence — to withdraw to a cell, a court, a training camp, a monastery, a studio — and to accept that the withdrawn person is now living by different rules to everyone else. Every serious practice regime begins with an exit. This is why intensive training so often strains relationships and why the returning practitioner is never quite reabsorbed; the withdrawal was structural, not incidental.

The second is stranger and more interesting. Sloterdijk describes practice as taking place in what he calls an auto-operatively curved space: a space in which the thing you are working on is the same thing that is doing the work. In almost every other kind of labour, the worker and the material are distinct. In practice they are identical, and the feedback loop closes on itself. That is why self-improvement is peculiarly unstable and peculiarly compelling: every act of improvement also alters the instrument doing the improving. From this he asks his best question, posed in the outlook as cur homo artista — why is the human an artist? His answer is that we are not artists because we make beautiful objects. We are artists because we are the material we work on.

The complementary idea is the entanglement of perfection. Once a standard of completeness exists, the practitioner is caught in a story with no natural ending: there is always another degree of refinement, and the pursuit of the complete is what keeps the incomplete practising. Perfection, in other words, functions less as a destination than as an engine.

Which brings us to the historical spine of the book, and the part that says most about the world we actually live in. Modernity, Sloterdijk argues, did not abolish asceticism. It nationalised it.

The ascetic impulse — withdraw, subject yourself to a programme, work on your own substance — did not evaporate when Europe secularised. It changed address. It moved out of the monastery and into the school, the gymnasium, the clinic, the barracks, the factory, and above all into the state's ambitions for its population. Private salvation became public policy. The demand to change your life became a demand to change life in general: to raise literacy, improve public health, build fitter citizens, produce better workers, remake entire societies through education, hygiene and physical culture. What had been a technique for saving one soul became a technique for processing millions.

His cleanest single example is Pierre de Coubertin, who revived the Olympic Games at the end of the nineteenth century. Here is an explicitly modern, explicitly secular institution, founded on something that looks a great deal like religious devotion: a flame, an oath, a ritual calendar, a cult of the perfected body, and a demand for years of unpaid, unglamorous training in the service of a few minutes of performance. At the other end of the same chapter Sloterdijk places L. Ron Hubbard, who built a movement out of graded exercises and audited progress through levels. The reason those two names sit together is the argument's punchline: exercise systems flourish exactly where doctrine has evaporated. Remove the gods and the drills remain, looking for something to attach themselves to.

There is one more theoretical layer, and it is the one that turns cultural history into a claim about the present. Sloterdijk proposes what he calls a general immunology, offered — ambitiously, and this is the part sympathetic reviewers hesitate over — as a replacement for metaphysics.

Human beings, he argues, defend themselves on three levels at once. There is the biological immune system, which handles infection and injury. There is a social one: solidarity, law, insurance, kinship, the arrangements by which people cover one another against misfortune. And there is a symbolic one: ritual, philosophy, art, religion, the practices that inoculate us against meaninglessness and against the knowledge that we are going to die. Exercise systems are how that third layer gets constructed and maintained. Practice, on this account, is not self-indulgence or self-improvement in the shallow sense. It is immune work. It is how a creature that knows it will die goes on functioning.

From that he derives his one genuine political proposal. Ecological threat, he argues, cannot be met by any society immunising itself alone, because the exposure is shared and no wall is high enough. What is required is co-immunity: a practice regime for the whole species. Note carefully that he is not asking for a shared belief, a global religion or a common ideology. He is asking for a shared discipline — a set of things everybody does, whatever they think. It is the moment where the entire apparatus of the book is meant to pay off, and it is genuinely interesting, because it treats the climate problem as a training problem rather than a persuasion problem.

It helps to place this book against two things a reader is more likely to have encountered.

The first is the modern habit shelf — the genre that explains how to make a behaviour automatic through cues, small steps and environmental design. Sloterdijk is doing the exact opposite job with the same subject matter. The habit books supply method and skip the question of what practice is or why humans are the kind of creature formed by it; Sloterdijk supplies precisely that account and offers no method whatsoever. Read side by side they are unusually complementary, and the comparison also exposes what is missing from each. The habit literature cannot tell you why you want the thing you are training for. Sloterdijk cannot tell you how to train.

The second comparison is older and more flattering to him. Marcus Aurelius wrote the Meditations as a private notebook, drilling himself nightly on anger, grief and mortality, and never intended a reader. Read as philosophy it is thin and unoriginal. Read the way Sloterdijk teaches you to read it — as a training document, a set of exercises rehearsed rather than propositions argued — it becomes the most complete surviving specimen of exactly what this book describes. That is the strongest evidence for the thesis: it makes texts that looked feeble as arguments legible as something else, and something better. Whole libraries of devotional and philosophical writing stop being bad theory and start being good drill.

He arrives at this from a serious lineage, and he names it. Wittgenstein on how rule-following is learned by training rather than by grasping a rule; Bourdieu on habitus, the socially deposited dispositions that make people act as their class and upbringing dispose them to act. What Sloterdijk adds is the vertical dimension. Bourdieu's habitus explains how people reproduce what they already are; vertical tension is meant to explain how anyone ever climbs out of it.

So how good is it? Here is the honest ledger, and it is decidedly mixed.

Start with the method, because everything else follows from it. This book proves nothing. That is a description rather than an insult. Sloterdijk works by erudite association: he reads Rilke against Nietzsche against Kafka against Cioran against Wittgenstein against Bourdieu, with real skill and enormous range, and at no point does a claim appear that could come out false. There is no test the thesis could fail. Worse, because he defines religion as practice, very nearly any repeated human activity counts as evidence for him — which is precisely why readers describe the theme as a catch-all that absorbs every subject it touches, and why one reader called the book a monumental flop suffering from an out-of-focus thesis. When a frame explains everything equally well, it has stopped doing explanatory work and started doing decorative work.

The sharpest criticism is political, and the philosopher Nina Power has made it most effectively. Her central objection is that the self at the heart of this book — the self that surveys its own existence and resolves to operate upon it — is assumed rather than examined. For a book that historicises everything else, she says, that self is remarkably underhistoricised. And the life being quietly celebrated turns out to be a very particular one: contemplative, unhurried, self-directed, belonging to an educated class with time to spare. Strip away the erudition, Power argues, and the practical residue amounts to pulling your socks up, with nothing at all said about the people whose days are consumed by paid work, by money, or by the care of other human beings. She goes further, and the comparison stings: this is philosophy that rhymes with Thatcherism, self-reliance recommended without reference to conditions.

She also notices something that is difficult to unsee afterwards. In a five-hundred-page book about how human beings form themselves through practice, women are almost entirely absent. Simone Weil and Hannah Arendt receive brief mentions. That is very close to all. A history of practice that omits the overwhelming majority of the practising — the people who have historically done most of the repetitive, disciplined, unglamorous work of keeping other humans alive — has a hole in it that no amount of erudition fills. And Power notes the ordering of his concerns: dullness, dejection and obsession appear to trouble Sloterdijk more than exploitation, dispossession and war do.

There is also the question of where the historical argument goes. The move from individual asceticism to state programmes for remaking whole populations passes uncomfortably close to the twentieth century's worst projects, and the book does less than it should to mark that boundary. This is an author with previous form on exactly this terrain: his 1999 lecture on genetic selection, delivered under the title Rules for the Human Park, provoked a furious public quarrel with Jürgen Habermas, who read it as flirting with eugenics, while Sloterdijk insisted that the subject could not be discussed at all under post-war German taboos. In 2009 he argued that the welfare state amounts to fiscal kleptocracy and should be replaced by voluntary giving. The charge of elitism has followed him for decades, and it lands on this book as squarely as on the interventions.

The prose divides readers absolutely. One specialist reviewer praises the pleasure of the text, the vigour of the writing, the novelty of the language, and argues that philosophy ought to be the poetry of language. Another calls it stodgy and long-winded, admits candidly that he is unsure he grasped the whole argument, and suspects the sheer volume of prose sometimes defeated the translator. A third observes that this idiosyncratic, lavishly exuberant, intensely literary style is a substantial part of why Sloterdijk took so long to be read seriously in English at all — he is, in that reviewer's phrase, strangely immune to academic appropriation. Ordinary readers report the same split from the inside: long stretches described as infuriating and tedious, punctuated without warning by passages of distilled poetic beauty. One reader reported taking thirteen pages of notes to get through the first quarter.

The structure compounds this. Roughly five hundred pages, more than twenty chapters, arranged as an introduction, three long parts and an outlook — and built as a sequence of chapter-length essays on individual figures rather than as a stepwise argument. The thesis arrives early and is complete early. What follows is accumulation rather than development, which is why the reading experience is lateral: you are not being carried towards a conclusion, you are being shown further instances of something already established.

It rates five point four out of ten — limited. The components are worth separating, because the average conceals a genuinely interesting shape. Explanatory power scores well: the practice-not-belief frame really does reorganise a mass of material, and reviewers across the political spectrum agree on that. Insight scores well too, because religions do not exist is a properly non-obvious move, and vertical tension names something most accounts of motivation miss entirely. Evidence and reasoning score poorly, for the reasons already given. Completeness scores poorly, given the missing counterarguments and the missing women. Practical utility is low, because there is no method here. And efficiency is the weakest of all: one idea restated across five hundred pages rather than developed across them. The score judges the ideas as delivered on the page and the craft of that delivery, and this book is considerably better at the first than at the second.

So what should you actually do?

If you read philosophy for its own sake, read it. The chapters on Rilke, Nietzsche, Unthan and Kafka are genuinely fine pieces of writing, the historical sweep is the pleasure being offered, and there is no substitute for the accumulation if the accumulation is what you want. Read it in print rather than listening to it, and read it by dipping: the chapter-essay structure supports that, the terminology needs re-reading, and the digressions are close to impossible to follow in audio. Expect to abandon it at least once.

Everybody else should take the idea and leave the book, because the idea is entirely portable and the apparatus is not required to carry it. Three things are worth keeping.

First: watch what you actually rehearse rather than what you believe. The repetitions are what shape you, and beliefs mostly follow the drills rather than driving them. If you want to know what someone is becoming, look at their week, not their opinions.

Second: notice the height you are currently looking up at. That perceived gap is what makes difficulty attractive rather than merely painful, which means the practical question is not how to find more willpower but whether anything is above you at all. If nothing is, nothing will pull, and no amount of self-discipline will substitute.

Third, and most useful: ask who set the regime you are following. Schools, employers, apps and governments all issue training programmes, and almost none of them announce themselves as such. Sloterdijk's real gift is that once you have seen practice as a set of programmes with authors, you cannot stop noticing who wrote yours, and what they wanted from you when they did.

There is a fourth thing, harder to act on but worth carrying. Sloterdijk's closing move is that the practices which once saved individual souls are now the only plausible mechanism for collective survival — that facing a shared, planetary exposure will require shared drills rather than shared convictions. Whether or not the philosophy underneath it holds, that is an unusually clear-eyed way to think about any large problem: stop asking what people need to believe, and start asking what they would have to do repeatedly, and who would set the standard. It is a question about training rather than persuasion, and most public argument would be improved by it.

If you want a method, a plan, or anything at all you can do on Monday morning, this is emphatically the wrong book; it is a lens and not an instruction manual, and it will frustrate anyone who arrives expecting technique. It is also, by a considerable margin, longer than its argument requires, and honest readers of it admit as much. But it is a good lens, and a rare one: a serious account of self-improvement that neither moralises about character nor reduces the whole business to psychology. And Rilke's headless statue goes on issuing its command long after you have put the book down — which may be the most persuasive evidence Sloterdijk offers that he is right about how these things work.
