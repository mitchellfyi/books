Some time around 1908, Rainer Maria Rilke stood in a Paris museum in front of a piece of broken marble: an archaic torso of Apollo, no head, no arms, no eyes. He wrote a poem about it, and the poem ends with a line that has been irritating readers for over a century. Looking at this mutilated stone, he writes, there is no place that does not see you. And then, without warning or explanation: you must change your life.

Peter Sloterdijk has built a five-hundred-page book on that sentence. What fascinates him is its structure. Here is an absolute command — not advice, not encouragement, a command — and there is nobody issuing it. No god, no priest, no law. A headless piece of stone somehow makes a demand on you, and you feel it. That, he argues, is the pure form of every imperative to improve that human beings have ever obeyed. Strip away the theology, the ideology and the motivational packaging, and what is left is this: a felt order to become something you are not yet.

Sloterdijk is a German philosopher, born in Karlsruhe in 1947, who studied at Munich and Hamburg, spent two years at an ashram in Pune at the end of the 1970s, and then wrote a book called Critique of Cynical Reason that became one of the best-selling works of philosophy in post-war Germany. He ran the Karlsruhe University of Arts and Design for fourteen years and co-hosted a philosophy programme on German television. He is, in other words, a public intellectual with a taste for provocation, and this book contains one of his best.

The provocation is this: religions do not exist.

He does not mean that people are not religious. He means that "religion" is the wrong unit of analysis. Look at what a religious tradition actually consists of, moment by moment, and you do not find a set of propositions people affirm. You find drills. You find fasting. You find prayer at fixed hours, five times a day or seven. You find repetition, submission to a rule, a novitiate, a teacher who has been doing this longer than you and who corrects your form. Doctrine, on this account, is not the substance of the tradition. Doctrine is what practitioners say about the practice afterwards, when someone asks them to explain it.

Once you see that, the categories reshuffle. A Benedictine monastery, a Stoic school in ancient Athens, a yoga lineage, a military academy and a modern fitness regime stop looking like different kinds of thing. They are all exercise systems: structured programmes for working on a human being until that human being is changed. And the creature at the centre of all of them is, as Sloterdijk puts it, constitutionally unfinished. We are the animal that has to train. He gives the techniques a name — anthropotechnics, the technologies of self-formation — and then rereads several thousand years of history as the record of them.

That gives him his sharpest single tool, and it is the part of the book most worth stealing. He calls it vertical tension.

The question is: why would anyone volunteer for difficulty? Training is unpleasant. Nobody enjoys the fourth hour. Sloterdijk's answer is that practice begins the moment you perceive a gap — a difference between where you currently stand and a more accomplished state that you can somehow sense above you but cannot yet reach. Cultures organise themselves around such heights: the saint, the master, the virtuoso, the champion. The pull is not moral pressure. It is more like gravity working upwards. His emblem for the person caught in it is the acrobat, suspended between the merely human and something beyond it, held up by nothing but practice.

His most striking illustration is a man called Carl Hermann Unthan, born in the nineteenth century without arms, who learned to play the violin with his feet, and to shoot, and to write, and who spent his life as a performer. Sloterdijk is not telling this story to inspire you. He is using it as the clearest available specimen of what he thinks everyone is doing all the time, only less visibly: taking something that ought to be impossible and grinding it down into a routine.

If practice needs a height, it also needs someone standing at it. A long stretch of the book is devoted to the figure of the trainer — the master, the coach, the abbot, the guru — and to what Sloterdijk calls master games. The trainer's job is not to teach content. It is to guarantee that the exaggeration is possible: to have gone further up than you, and to keep insisting that the next impossible thing is merely difficult. This is why every exercise system produces hierarchies and why leaving one feels like apostasy. And it gives him a neat account of upheaval: a great deal of what we call revolution, he suggests, is really a change of trainer. The demand to practise persists; only the authority setting the drills is replaced.

There is a darker current too, and the book is better for it. Kafka's hunger artists — performers who starve themselves in public until the crowd loses interest — appear as the reductio of the practising life: discipline perfected to the point where it consumes the practitioner and serves nobody. The Romanian pessimist Cioran, whom Sloterdijk reads as a kind of Parisian Buddhist, supplies the other version: exercise as an elegant way of withdrawing from life rather than intensifying it. These chapters matter because they stop the argument being a straightforward celebration. Practice can hollow you out. Exercises can go wrong, and a good deal of what people submit to is, in his phrase, misexercise.

That thread comes to rest in one of his best formulations: culture itself operates as a monastic rule. Every society issues a schedule — when you rise, what you repeat, what counts as slacking — and enforces it without ever writing it down as a rule. We do not experience this as discipline because it arrived as normality.

Then comes the historical argument, which is where the book gets genuinely interesting about the world we actually live in. Modernity, Sloterdijk says, did not abolish asceticism. It nationalised it.

The ascetic impulse — withdraw, discipline yourself, subject your body to a programme — did not disappear when Europe secularised. It changed address. It moved out of the monastery and into the school, the gymnasium, the clinic, the barracks, the factory, and above all into the state's ambitions for its population. The demand to change your life became a demand to change life in general: to improve public health, to raise literacy, to build fitter citizens, to remake whole societies through education and hygiene and physical culture. Private salvation became a public programme.

His cleanest example is Pierre de Coubertin, who revived the Olympic Games at the end of the nineteenth century — an explicitly modern, explicitly secular institution, founded on something that looks a great deal like religious devotion to training, complete with flame, oath, ritual and a cult of the perfected body. At the other end of the same chapter he puts L. Ron Hubbard, who assembled a movement out of graded exercises and audited progress. The point of putting those two names in one chapter is that exercise systems flourish precisely where doctrine has evaporated. Take away the gods and the drills remain, looking for somewhere to attach themselves.

There is one more layer, and it is the one that turns the book from cultural history into an argument about now. Sloterdijk proposes what he calls a general immunology. Human beings, he says, defend themselves on three levels. There is the biological immune system, which handles infection. There is a social one — solidarity, law, insurance, the arrangements by which people cover each other against misfortune. And there is a symbolic one: ritual, philosophy, art, the practices that inoculate us against meaninglessness and against the knowledge that we die. Exercise systems are how that third layer gets built and maintained. He offers this, ambitiously, as a replacement for metaphysics.

And it leads to his one real political proposal. Ecological threat, he argues, cannot be handled by any society immunising itself alone; it requires what he calls co-immunity — a shared practice regime for the whole species. Not a shared belief. A shared discipline. It is the moment where the whole apparatus of the book is supposed to pay off.

So does it? Here is the honest ledger, and it is mixed.

The book proves nothing. That is not a cheap shot; it is a description of the method. Sloterdijk works by erudite association — Rilke, Nietzsche, Kafka's hunger artists, Cioran, Wittgenstein, Bourdieu — reading texts against each other with great skill and no testable claim in sight. There is no result that would come out differently if the thesis were false. And because he defines religion as practice, almost any repeated activity qualifies as evidence for him, which is why even sympathetic readers describe the theme as a catch-all that swallows every subject it touches.

The sharpest criticism is political, and it comes from the philosopher Nina Power. Her objection is that the self at the centre of this book — the self that surveys its own life and decides to operate on it — is assumed rather than examined. It is, she says, remarkably underhistoricised for a book that historicises everything else. And the life being celebrated is a specific one: contemplative, unhurried, belonging to an educated class with time to spare. Strip away the erudition, she argues, and the practical advice amounts to pulling your socks up, with nothing said about the people whose days are consumed by paid work, money worries or caring for somebody else. She also points out something hard to unsee once noticed: in five hundred pages about how human beings form themselves, women are almost entirely absent. Simone Weil and Hannah Arendt get brief mentions. That is close to it. And she notes his priorities — that dullness, dejection and obsession seem to trouble him more than exploitation, dispossession and war.

There is also the matter of where the historical argument leads. The move from individual asceticism to state programmes for remaking whole populations passes very close to the twentieth century's worst projects, and the book does less than it should to mark that boundary clearly. This is an author with form here: his 1999 lecture on genetic selection provoked a bitter public quarrel with Jürgen Habermas, and in 2009 he argued that the welfare state amounts to fiscal kleptocracy and should give way to voluntary giving. The charge of elitism has followed him for decades, and it lands on this book too.

The prose divides readers completely. One specialist reviewer praises the pleasure of the text, the vigour of the writing, the novelty of the language, and means it. Another calls it stodgy and long-winded, admits he is not certain he grasped the whole thing, and suspects the sheer volume of prose overwhelmed the translator in places. A third notes that this style is precisely why Sloterdijk took so long to be read seriously in English at all. All three are describing the same book, and readers report the same split: long infuriating stretches, punctuated by passages of real beauty.

It rates five point four out of ten — limited. Strong on explanatory reach and on insight, because the central move genuinely reorganises the material. Weak on evidence, weak on completeness, and weakest of all on efficiency: this is one idea restated across five hundred pages, not one idea developed across them. The score judges the ideas and the writing, and this book is much better at the first than the second.

So, the decision. If you read philosophy for its own sake, read it — the readings of Rilke, Nietzsche and Kafka are worth the ticket, and the historical sweep is the pleasure on offer. Read it in print, dipping into chapters, because the essay structure suits that and the digressions are hard to follow any other way.

Everyone else should take the idea and leave the book, because the idea is portable and the apparatus is not required to carry it. Three things are worth keeping. Watch what you actually rehearse rather than what you believe, because the repetitions are what shape you, and beliefs mostly follow the drills rather than driving them. Notice the height you are currently looking up at, because that perceived gap is what makes difficulty attractive rather than merely painful — and if nothing is above you, nothing will pull. And ask who set the regime you are following, because schools, employers and governments all issue training programmes and rarely announce them as such. That last question is the book's real gift: once you see practice as programmes with authors, you start noticing who wrote yours.

If you want a method, a plan, or anything you can do on Monday morning, this is emphatically the wrong book. It is a lens, not an instruction manual. But it is a good lens, and Rilke's headless statue keeps making its demand long after you have put the thing down.
