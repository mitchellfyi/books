How Not to Age asks how much of age-related disease and lost function can be delayed. Michael Greger's answer is that genes explain only part of lifespan variation and that diet and lifestyle influence many processes involved in ageing. His preferred intervention is a whole-food plant-based diet, supported by activity, sleep, weight control, stress management, and social ties.

The book is four books in one. First, it maps eleven pathways: AMPK and mTOR nutrient sensing, autophagy or cellular recycling, senescent cells, epigenetics, glycation, IGF-1, inflammation, oxidation, sirtuins, and telomeres. These are real areas of ageing science. They interact, and no one marker represents the whole process.

The critical distinction is between mechanism and outcome. A food may change a molecule in cells, extend life in worms, or improve a biomarker in a short trial without making humans live longer or function better. Mechanisms generate hypotheses. Human clinical outcomes determine confidence. The book's thousands of citations make claims traceable, but do not make every study design equal.

Part two examines Mediterranean, Okinawan, Blue Zone, and plant-based patterns. The dependable signal is not one berry or bean. It is a pattern rich in whole grains, legumes, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds, with less highly processed food, processed meat, salt, and sugary drinks. Greger argues for excluding animal foods. The broader evidence supports plant-predominant diets but does not prove that full exclusion is necessary; fish, for example, has favourable associations in several evidence reviews.

Centenarians offer clues, not experiments. Long-lived communities combine food, movement, social ties, purpose, genetics, healthcare, material conditions, and history. Validating someone's age does not reveal which habit caused it.

Part three is the most useful reference section. It covers bones, bowel and bladder function, circulation, hair, hearing, hormones, immunity, joints, mind, muscle, sex, skin, teeth, vision, and dignity in death. Its best insight is that healthspan means preserving capability, not merely adding years. Diet matters, but so do resistance and aerobic activity, balance, vaccination, dental care, hearing aids, vision care, rehabilitation, screening, treatment, and relationships.

Part four presents Greger's Anti-Aging Eight: nuts, greens, berries, xenohormesis and microRNA manipulation, prebiotics and postbiotics, calorie restriction, protein restriction, and preserving NAD+. The first three are clear foods inside a healthy pattern. The later categories mix intriguing biology with limited human outcome evidence. A memorable list makes unlike levels of evidence appear more equal than they are.

The largest safety issue is restriction. Lower calories and certain amino acids extend life in some model organisms and affect nutrient-sensing pathways. Human lifespan benefit is unproven. In a two-year trial of younger non-obese adults, calorie restriction reduced fat but also fat-free mass. Older adults face higher risks from frailty, falls, poor healing, and malnutrition. Geriatric nutrition groups commonly recommend at least one to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram daily for healthy older adults, often more during activity or illness. Needs vary, but preserving actual muscle and strength outranks theoretical pathway optimisation.

Greger is usefully sceptical of expensive supplements, hormones, collagen, and anti-ageing procedures. A concentrated compound can fail even when the food containing it is healthy. Spermidine, for example, supports autophagy theory, but a twelve-month randomised supplement trial did not improve its primary memory outcome. Fully plant-based eating also needs planning, particularly for vitamin B12 and sometimes calcium, iodine, vitamin D, iron, zinc, and omega-three status.

The book earns credit for breadth and for reporting many failed products. Its weakness is priority. Six hundred-plus pages, hundreds of links, and obscure null interventions bury a compact core. Use it as an idea index, not a uniform prescription.

If you read it, start with the function that matters to you, then inspect the direct evidence and compare the proposed food with simpler clinical or behavioural options. Do not attempt to perform every recommendation. A short, varied routine that preserves nutrition and capacity has greater value than an elaborate regimen built from preliminary targets.

That core is strong: do not smoke; eat a varied, minimally processed, plant-rich diet; move regularly; train strength and balance; protect sleep and connection; maintain hearing, vision, teeth, cognition, and mobility through appropriate care; and correct real nutritional needs. Before adding any intervention, ask whether the evidence comes from cells, animals, biomarkers, observation, or human outcomes. The aim is not to win a molecular score. It is to remain capable, connected, and adequately nourished for as much of life as possible.
