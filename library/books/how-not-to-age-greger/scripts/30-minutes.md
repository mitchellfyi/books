How Not to Age is Michael Greger's attempt to answer an enormous question: how much of the disease, weakness, and lost independence associated with getting older can be delayed? He is not offering immortality. His useful target is healthspan, meaning more years with enough physical, cognitive, sensory, and social function to live well.

The title still makes a bold promise. Everybody ages, and no diet has been shown to stop that process in humans. Greger's narrower argument is more defensible. Genes explain only part of variation in lifespan. Bodies respond to food, movement, sleep, stress, infection, treatment, and social conditions. Those inputs can change disease risk and the timing of functional decline.

Greger is a physician and nutrition communicator who founded NutritionFacts.org. His settled position is that a whole-food plant-based diet offers the best foundation for health. In this book, he works backwards from anti-ageing biology and asks whether diet can influence each pathway. He concludes that it can, then adds centenarian lifestyles and an organ-by-organ survey.

The result is four books in one. Part one covers eleven cellular pathways. Part two constructs an optimal diet and lifestyle. Part three examines preservation of fifteen functions, from bones to dignity in death. Part four offers an Anti-Aging Eight. It is a six-hundred-page reference system disguised as a continuous health book.

Part one is the technical foundation. Greger selects A. M. P. K., autophagy, cellular senescence, epigenetics, glycation, IGF-1, inflammation, em-tor, oxidation, sirtuins, and telomeres. Current biogerontology uses overlapping frameworks and has proposed twelve hallmarks of ageing. No list is a final set of independent switches. The processes interact.

A. M. P. K. is part of cellular energy sensing. It responds when energy is scarce and encourages processes that generate energy rather than store it. Em-tor helps cells respond to nutrients, amino acids, and growth signals. Growth is necessary, but permanently elevated growth signalling may trade repair and longevity against development and reproduction. These pathways make fasting, calorie restriction, and protein restriction attractive to longevity researchers.

Attraction is not proof. A pathway can be essential without having one universally desirable setting. An older adult recovering from infection may need energy and protein to rebuild tissue. Suppressing growth signals might sound anti-ageing while undermining muscle and healing. The right response depends on state, timing, tissue, and outcome.

Autophagy means self-eating. Cells break down damaged structures and recycle components. The process is vital housekeeping, and reduced autophagy is associated with ageing. Fasting and exercise can increase related signals. Greger looks for foods and compounds, including spermidine, that may support this recycling.

The evidence ladder matters immediately. Spermidine extends life or affects autophagy in model organisms and occurs in wheat germ, mushrooms, and tempeh. A twelve-month randomised trial of a spermidine-rich supplement in older adults with subjective cognitive decline did not improve the primary memory outcome. The mechanism remains interesting; a proven cognitive or lifespan benefit does not follow.

Cellular senescence refers to cells that stop dividing. Senescence can suppress cancer and assist wound responses, but accumulating senescent cells can release inflammatory signals and disrupt tissue. “Zombie cells” is a memorable label that makes clearance sound obviously beneficial. Yet removing a complex cell state safely in a living human is not equivalent to eating a quercetin-rich onion.

Epigenetics concerns regulation layered onto the DNA sequence. Chemical marks and chromatin structure help determine which genes are active. Epigenetic clocks can estimate patterns associated with age, but a lower clock reading is not automatically a longer or healthier life. A marker can predict risk without being the cause, and changing the marker may not change the outcome.

Glycation occurs when sugars bind non-enzymatically to proteins, lipids, or DNA, creating products that can stiffen or damage tissue. Persistent high blood glucose increases this burden. Good glucose control, dietary quality, activity, and appropriate treatment therefore matter. The concept does not mean every carbohydrate ages the body equally; beans, intact grains, fruit, and sugary drinks have different structures and effects.

IGF-1 and em-tor illustrate the growth trade-off. Higher growth signalling can support development, muscle, and recovery while potentially influencing cancer or ageing pathways. Animal longevity from reducing particular amino acids cannot be copied directly into older humans. The goal is not the lowest achievable protein or growth factor. It is enough anabolic support without chronic metabolic excess.

Inflammation also has two faces. Acute inflammation fights infection and supports repair. Exercise causes temporary inflammatory signalling that participates in adaptation. Chronic, poorly resolved inflammation is associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic illness, neurodegeneration, and frailty. A food may lower a blood marker without proving that it prevents those outcomes.

Oxidative stress is similarly easy to oversimplify. Reactive molecules can damage cellular components, but they also transmit signals and help the body adapt. Large antioxidant doses have not reliably extended life and some supplements have caused harm. The objective is not to eliminate oxidation. It is to maintain a resilient system of exposure, signalling, defence, and repair.

Sirtuins are enzymes involved in metabolism, stress responses, and gene regulation. They became famous through calorie restriction and resveratrol research. NAD+ participates in their activity and in cellular energy transfer. Age-related changes make NAD+ boosters commercially attractive. The biochemical story is much stronger than evidence that a consumer supplement extends healthy human life.

Telomeres protect chromosome ends and often shorten as cells divide. Short telomeres are associated with age and disease, but the relationship is not a simple countdown. Different tissues behave differently, measurement is noisy, and unlimited telomere maintenance can support cancer. A diet that correlates with longer telomeres has not thereby been shown to reverse ageing.

The pathway section teaches a valuable model: ageing is distributed across interacting systems, so a single miracle cure is implausible. It also creates the book's recurring mistake. When a plant compound touches a pathway, biological plausibility slides towards advice. A food can alter a cell, a worm, or a biomarker without changing human disability-free survival.

Use a strict hierarchy. Cell studies identify possible mechanisms. Animal studies allow invasive causal tests but may not translate. Human observational studies examine real long-term exposure but retain confounding. Trials improve causal inference, yet short trials often measure surrogate outcomes rather than dementia, fracture, disability, or death. Quantity of citations cannot erase those differences.

Part two moves from molecules to whole lives. Greger considers the Mediterranean diet, Okinawan diet, Blue Zones, Seventh-day Adventists, and centenarians. He then covers exercise, weight, sleep, stress, and social ties. These populations suggest that exceptional survival is shaped by patterns, not isolated molecules.

The repeated dietary pattern is rich in whole grains, legumes, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds. These foods provide fibre, micronutrients, unsaturated fats, and many phytochemicals. They also tend to replace processed meat, refined starch, excess salt, and sugary drinks. Healthy plant-based dietary indices associate with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, though much evidence remains observational.

Food quality matters more than the plant label. Crisps, sweets, refined bread, and sugary drinks can be vegan. A minimally processed plant diet differs metabolically and nutritionally. Greger knows this; his whole-food qualifier does important work. The practical unit is the pattern and substitution, not simply the absence of animal ingredients.

Greger takes a further step and argues that complete exclusion of animal foods is optimal. The evidence supports plant predominance more securely than that strict boundary. Mediterranean patterns can include fish and modest animal foods while producing favourable outcomes. An umbrella review found several moderate- or high-quality beneficial associations for fish, including all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.

That does not prove fish is required. Preparation, species, contaminants, sustainability, and what fish replaces all matter. It does mean that grouping fish with processed meat or treating any animal food as a concession made only for pleasure does not represent the whole evidence fairly.

A fully plant-based diet also requires planning. Vitamin B12 needs a reliable fortified source or supplement. Calcium, iodine, vitamin D, iron, zinc, and long-chain omega-three status can become concerns depending on food choice, fortification, absorption, location, and health. Meat eaters can be deficient too. The lesson is nutritional adequacy, not a contest between identities.

Centenarian stories are persuasive because they look like natural experiments. They are not. Long-lived populations differ in genes, migration, healthcare, climate, occupation, income, social organisation, cohort history, food access, and birth records. People who survive to one hundred are selected from everyone who shared the same culture and died earlier.

Blue Zone ages have been criticised and defended. Careful researchers cross-check documents to avoid mistaken identity and false age. Even perfectly validated ages do not isolate causes. Okinawans did not randomly receive sweet potatoes while a control group received another diet. Food, energy intake, work, war, infection, healthcare, and society changed together.

The reasonable inference is a bundle of low-risk habits: do not smoke, eat mostly nutritious plants, move as part of life, maintain social ties, and avoid chronic excess. The unreasonable inference is that one bean, beverage, or ritual caused longevity or that a modern reader can reproduce a population's survival by copying a menu.

Beverages show how easily one category becomes crowded. Water is the simple default. Sugary drinks add rapidly consumed energy and are consistently poor choices. Alcohol is not required for health, and observational claims about red wine are vulnerable to selection and confounding. Tea and coffee contain compounds of interest and often show favourable associations, but preparation, dose, caffeine response, sleep, medication, and what the drink replaces affect the result. No reader needs every beverage with one positive study.

Weight control also changes with age and context. Excess visceral fat can worsen diabetes, cardiovascular risk, mobility, and inflammation. Yet weight loss can remove muscle and bone as well as fat. Waist, strength, function, appetite, trajectory, and disease matter more than pursuing the lowest scale number. Preventing unplanned weight loss in an older adult can be a healthier goal than activating an energy-scarcity pathway.

Exercise appears in part two but deserves more weight. Aerobic activity improves cardiorespiratory capacity, glucose regulation, blood pressure, mood, and daily endurance. Resistance work provides the direct signal to preserve muscle and strength. Balance training and environmental changes reduce falls. Activity supports function even if an exact number of added years remains uncertain.

Associations between leisure exercise and lifespan can be confounded: healthy, wealthy people may be more able to exercise. Hard occupational labour can correlate with worse health because it combines repetition, low control, stress, injury, and poor recovery. Those complexities do not erase experimental evidence for fitness and functional outcomes. They tell us to measure capacity and health, not treat every exertion as equivalent.

Sleep, stress, and relationships complete the regimen. Chronic sleep difficulty affects function and disease risk, while exact needs vary. Stress management matters because chronic threat affects behaviour, sleep, blood pressure, and inflammation. Social connection supports wellbeing, care, meaning, and survival, though it cannot be reduced to one molecule or daily quota.

These habits operate through environments. Safe streets make walking possible. Money and kitchens affect food. Work schedules affect sleep. Housing affects heat, falls, and isolation. Caregiving consumes time and energy. Medical access changes whether blood pressure, hearing loss, cataract, depression, or cancer is detected and treated. A personal checklist can help, but it should not turn unequal conditions into individual blame.

Part three is the book's longest section and its strongest reason to exist as a reference. Greger asks how to preserve bones, bowel and bladder function, circulation, hair, hearing, hormones, immune function, joints, mind, muscles, sex life, skin, teeth, vision, and dignity. Healthy ageing becomes concrete.

The bones chapter must be joined to muscle and falls. Bone needs loading, adequate energy, calcium, vitamin D, hormones, and sometimes medication. Muscle and balance affect whether a stumble becomes a fracture. Vision, footwear, obstacles, and sedating drugs affect falls. A food-only answer misses the system.

Weight loss can improve metabolic risk while reducing loading, muscle, and bone. The same change can help one outcome and harm another. An older person with obesity and preserved muscle presents a different problem from an underweight person losing appetite. “Lower is better” fails when the objective is resilience.

Bowel and bladder function affect dignity and participation. Fibre, fluid, movement, medications, pelvic health, infection, and disease can all matter. Greger finds food interventions such as cranberries, but persistent symptoms need assessment. A berry cannot determine whether the cause is obstruction, infection, neurological disease, medication, or pelvic-floor dysfunction.

Circulation ties the book back to dietary patterns. Blood pressure, lipids, glucose, smoking, activity, medication, and vascular disease affect nearly every organ. Plant-rich diets can improve several risk factors. It is a mistake to turn that value into rejection of effective medicines or emergency care. Lifestyle and treatment are often complementary, not competing ideologies.

Hair is emotionally important but biologically lower priority than circulation or cognition. Greger considers greying, loss, diet, and reversible causes. Genetics dominates many patterns. Thyroid disease, iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, medication, autoimmune disease, and stress may require diagnosis. The chapter demonstrates the attraction and cost of covering every visible sign of age.

Hearing has larger consequences than appearance. Untreated hearing loss can reduce communication, confidence, and social life and is associated with cognitive decline. Nutrition may affect risk at the margins, but hearing assessment, protection from damaging noise, and hearing aids have direct functional relevance. Compensation is a successful healthspan intervention even when it does not make an ear biologically younger.

Hormone chapters are fertile ground for anti-ageing marketing. Testosterone, oestrogen, growth hormone, and other treatments can have valid indications and real risks. A low measurement does not always cause a symptom, and raising a value does not guarantee benefit. The sensible path is diagnosis, shared decisions, and outcome monitoring rather than pursuing youthful numbers.

Immune ageing increases vulnerability to infection and reduces vaccine response, while chronic inflammation can also rise. Nutrition, sleep, activity, and disease control support immunity, but vaccination, infection prevention, and timely treatment remain central. Calling a food immune-boosting is usually less informative than asking which infection or clinical outcome it changes.

Joints combine cartilage, bone, muscle, tendons, pain processing, injury, and inflammation. Movement can strengthen support and preserve range; excessive or poorly progressed load can worsen symptoms. Weight, sleep, mood, work, prior injury, and arthritis type matter. Collagen powders and food extracts should not displace assessment, graded exercise, pain management, or indicated treatment.

The mind chapter receives the most space because dementia is one of the greatest fears of ageing. Vascular health, smoking, activity, education, hearing, sleep, social connection, depression, diabetes, and blood pressure contribute to risk. No food guarantees prevention, and association between one nutrient and cognition can reflect the rest of a person's life.

This chapter is most useful when it integrates risks and weakest when a small food trial becomes a memorable directive. A person worried about cognitive change needs assessment, not an escalating stack of berries, spices, and supplements. Preventive habits are worthwhile precisely because they have multiple benefits, not because they guarantee an intact mind.

Muscle is where the book's longevity mechanisms collide with geriatric reality. Ageing muscles respond less strongly to the same protein and training signal. Inactivity, illness, bed rest, inflammation, and inadequate energy accelerate loss. Reduced strength then limits movement, which creates further decline.

Resistance training is the direct intervention. It can improve strength and function even in later life. Protein supports the response. Expert geriatric groups commonly recommend at least one to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram daily for healthy older adults, with higher needs during activity or illness. Distribution, food quality, kidney function, total energy, and individual goals affect the plan.

Greger is interested in protein restriction because lower amino-acid signalling extends life in some animals and may reduce IGF-1 or em-tor. The translation is uncertain. A frail person losing muscle has an immediate, human outcome that outweighs a theoretical pathway benefit. Plant protein can meet needs, but low-protein ideology should not obscure adequacy.

Sexual function depends on circulation, hormones, medication, relationships, mental health, pain, pelvic health, and chronic disease. Healthy diet and activity can improve contributing factors. Supplements marketed for desire or performance often rely on weak studies. Persistent change warrants a clinical and relational view rather than embarrassment or one food solution.

Skin reflects intrinsic ageing and external exposure. Smoking and ultraviolet radiation are major modifiable causes of visible damage. Sun protection has more direct relevance than collagen supplements or homemade serums. Nutrition supports skin as part of overall health, but cosmetic claims often measure small appearance changes over short periods.

Teeth and gums affect eating, pain, infection, speech, and confidence. Dietary sugar matters, but brushing with fluoride toothpaste, interdental cleaning where appropriate, and dental care are fundamental. A longevity book that moves quickly to foods without foregrounding these practices reverses the priority.

The bowel, bladder, teeth, hearing, and sex chapters also expose ageism. Problems that are common with age can be dismissed as inevitable or embarrassing even when assessment and treatment can help. Healthy ageing is not only preventing damage before it happens. It is taking present symptoms seriously, preserving participation, and using support without treating assistance as failure.

Vision chapters can connect diet to pigments such as lutein and zeaxanthin, yet eye disease is specific. Cataract, macular degeneration, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy have different mechanisms and treatments. Smoking avoidance, diabetes control, eye examinations, sun protection, and indicated therapy cannot be replaced by greens or goji berries.

Preserving dignity is an important closing function. Healthy ageing does not mean denying mortality. A good end can require pain and symptom control, privacy, choice, relationships, communication, and documented wishes. Advance care planning belongs beside diet because control and meaning matter even when biological decline cannot be reversed.

This ending also corrects the language of optimisation. A person can follow every lifestyle recommendation and still develop disease, disability, or dementia. Biology includes chance and limits. Care then changes form: relief, adaptation, communication, and companionship become the intervention. Dignity cannot depend on maintaining independence forever or earning health through perfect behaviour.

The function section's general lesson is that adaptation counts. Hearing aids, glasses, mobility supports, dental work, rehabilitation, home changes, and help from other people can preserve capability without changing an ageing pathway. Healthspan is an outcome in a real environment, not a molecular purity score.

Part four tries to convert the entire survey into eight memorable categories: nuts, greens, berries, xenohormesis and microRNA manipulation, prebiotics and postbiotics, calorie restriction, protein restriction, and NAD+ preservation. The uniform list hides unequal confidence.

Nuts, greens, and berries are nutritious foods with favourable evidence inside broader patterns. They add unsaturated fats, fibre, minerals, pigments, and other compounds. None is a unique lifespan medicine. Someone can eat a healthy varied diet without treating berries as superior to every other fruit or using a daily checklist as a moral score.

Xenohormesis proposes that stress compounds made by plants can trigger useful human defences. MicroRNAs regulate gene expression, and claims that dietary microRNAs create meaningful effects in humans remain technically and biologically difficult. These topics are worth studying. They are not yet instructions as clear as eat vegetables.

Prebiotics are substrates used by microbes; postbiotics are microbial products or preparations with potential effects. The microbiome responds to dietary pattern, drugs, infection, and many other factors. Fibre-rich diversity is a defensible default. Selecting a specific product to extend life requires far more direct evidence.

Calorie restriction has some of the most famous animal evidence in ageing. In a two-year trial of younger non-obese humans, participants achieved moderate restriction, lost fat, and improved some risk factors. They also lost fat-free mass. The trial did not show longer life and did not test frail older adults.

It is possible to increase lean mass as a percentage of body weight while losing actual muscle if fat falls faster. Calling that stronger would be wrong without strength evidence. Calorie restriction can also threaten bone, appetite, healing, and nutrient adequacy. Its risk-benefit balance changes radically with age, starting weight, disease, and frailty.

Protein restriction has the same problem. The molecular theory is coherent, but humans must maintain muscle, enzymes, immune proteins, skin, and repair. Needs rise in many older adults. The safest conclusion is not high protein for everyone or low protein for longevity. It is adequate protein, progressive resistance work, and personalised adjustment.

NAD+ completes the list with another mechanistic target. It is central to metabolism and declines in some tissues with age. Supplements can raise related metabolites without proving better function or survival. The farther a recommendation moves from food pattern and clinical outcome towards enzyme manipulation, the more scepticism it needs.

Greger deserves credit for applying substantial scepticism to commercial products. He examines resveratrol, hormones, collagen, antioxidant pills, and many other supposed breakthroughs. He repeatedly finds that higher doses can fail and that supplements cannot recreate the nutritional matrix of food.

The same standard must apply to his preferred interventions. Food-company funding can bias a berry study just as drug-company funding can bias a pill study. Advocacy and prior belief can affect search, framing, comparators, outcomes, and emphasis. Transparency about citations is necessary; adversarial selection is still required.

Relative risk needs translation too. A striking percentage can describe a small absolute difference, a short follow-up, or an unusual group. Multiple comparisons create chance findings. A study may measure a biomarker rather than a symptom, and statistical significance does not establish a meaningful effect. Before changing a routine, ask how many relevant people benefited, by how much, for how long, and at what cost.

The information architecture is the book's largest defect. Thousands of references sound rigorous, but a reader cannot retain hundreds of interventions. Links sometimes carry essential qualifications outside the book. Null results and obscure possibilities remain in the narrative. The same plant-based answer returns through many mechanisms.

This density creates false equality. A small trial of a powder can occupy the same paragraph space as smoking cessation or strength training. A molecular possibility can acquire the same memorable heading as a clinical practice. Without an explicit evidence and impact ranking, attention becomes the scarce resource the book spends poorly.

The most efficient reading method is selective. Start with a function or intervention that matters. Identify the outcome. Ask whether the evidence is mechanistic, animal, observational, or randomised in relevant humans. Check absolute effect, replication, duration, comparator, population, feasibility, cost, and harm. Look for a simpler intervention with more direct evidence.

Separate prevention from treatment. A healthy pattern may lower average risk without reversing an established condition, and an intervention useful after diagnosis may not benefit a healthy person. Separate deficiency correction from optimisation too. Vitamin B12 for someone without a reliable source is necessary nutrition; an exotic supplement intended to raise a normal biomarker is a different proposition. Clear categories prevent one positive result from expanding into universal use.

Time horizon matters. Better blood pressure can appear quickly and has established clinical meaning. Dementia prevention, disability-free survival, and lifespan require years or decades. When a short study uses an intermediate marker, confidence should shrink with the distance to the promised outcome. This is not cynicism. It is protection from spending years on an intervention whose evidence stops at a laboratory number.

Do not attempt everything. A routine that is nutritionally adequate, socially workable, affordable, and repeatable has more value than an elaborate protocol abandoned after a month. More foods, rules, and biomarkers can increase anxiety without improving function.

The book rates as worthwhile rather than excellent. Its explanation of multidimensional ageing is strong. Its breadth and source discovery are impressive. Its plant-rich foundation, anti-supplement scepticism, and function section offer real value. Its reasoning weakens when pathway plausibility becomes food advice and a plant-based prior narrows comparison.

Its practical value also needs safeguards. Restricting calories or protein can conflict with muscle, bone, appetite, and recovery. A strict plant diet needs nutrient planning. Exercise and strength deserve higher priority. Clinical care and social conditions cannot be reduced to personal food choice. Six hundred pages deliver a decision core that can fit comfortably inside fifteen minutes.

That core is this. Do not smoke. Build most meals from varied whole grains, legumes, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds. Limit highly processed food, processed meat, sugary drinks, and excess salt. If eating fully plant-based, secure vitamin B12 and assess other vulnerable nutrients.

Move regularly. Include aerobic activity, progressive strength work, and balance. Protect enough energy and protein to maintain muscle, healing, and resilience. Sleep, sustain relationships, and address persistent stress. Use vaccination, screening, dental care, hearing and vision support, rehabilitation, medication, and treatment when they improve function.

Be sceptical of supplements and anti-ageing products, but do not reserve scepticism only for commercial medicine. Apply it to attractive foods, mechanisms, Blue Zone stories, and the author's favoured pattern. A long reference list is an invitation to verify, not a command to obey.

Finally, judge success through life rather than a laboratory dashboard. Can you rise, carry, walk, hear, see, think, eat, connect, recover, and decide? Are symptoms treated and risks managed? Does the routine leave room for pleasure and other people? Those outcomes are closer to healthy ageing than manipulating every pathway.

How Not to Age is best understood as an unusually large catalogue of hypotheses surrounding a small, robust principle. Ageing has modifiable parts. Protect them through ordinary patterns before pursuing exotic targets. The purpose is not to make the body look molecularly young. It is to remain capable, connected, adequately nourished, and dignified for as much of a finite life as possible.
