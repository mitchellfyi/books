How Not to Age is Michael Greger's attempt to examine almost everything a person might do to live longer and remain capable. Its real subject is healthspan: not immortality, but delaying disease and preserving movement, cognition, independence, connection, and dignity.

Greger begins from two claims. Genes explain only part of variation in lifespan, so environment and behaviour matter. And ageing is not one clock but a network of cellular processes that can respond to what a body eats and does. From there, he argues that a whole-food plant-based diet can influence every major ageing pathway and reduce the chronic diseases that shorten healthy life.

The work is deliberately four books in one. Part one supplies molecular biology. Part two builds an overall diet and lifestyle from centenarian populations. Part three asks how to preserve particular functions. Part four compresses the proposed priorities into an Anti-Aging Eight.

The eleven pathways in part one are AMPK, autophagy, cellular senescence, epigenetics, glycation, IGF-1, inflammation, mTOR, oxidation, sirtuins, and telomeres. Several sit within recognised hallmarks of ageing, although current researchers divide the territory differently and describe twelve interacting hallmarks rather than one final list.

AMPK and mTOR help cells respond to energy and nutrients. Autophagy breaks down and recycles damaged material. Senescent cells stop dividing and can release inflammatory signals. Epigenetic changes alter which genes are active without rewriting DNA. Glycation links sugars to proteins and can damage their function. IGF-1 participates in growth. Oxidative stress, inflammation, sirtuins, and telomeres connect damage, repair, signalling, and cellular lifespan.

This map is valuable because it dismantles the idea of one cause and one cure. The same intervention can touch several systems; the systems also affect one another. It is dangerous when a pathway becomes a marketing bridge. A compound activating autophagy in cells, extending life in worms, or changing a human blood marker has not thereby been shown to extend human life.

That is the key reading rule for the entire book: mechanism generates a hypothesis; direct human outcomes set confidence. Animal work can test causality but may not translate. Observational studies can follow real lives but retain confounding and healthy-user bias. Randomised trials improve causal inference but may be short, small, and focused on surrogate markers. A list of thirteen thousand references remains a list of unequal evidence.

The pathway chapters repeatedly propose dietary levers. Energy scarcity can activate AMPK and autophagy while reducing growth signalling. Plants provide compounds that may affect inflammation, oxidation, senescence, or gene regulation. Lower blood sugar can reduce glycation. These links are reasonable directions for research, but pathway language creates false precision when the actual intervention is simply a healthier diet. A person does not need to manipulate every enzyme consciously for their body to respond to food and movement.

Oxidation illustrates the trap. Reactive molecules can damage cells, yet they also carry signals that help adaptation. Large antioxidant doses have not reliably extended life and some trials have found harm. The goal is not to erase oxidation. It is to support a system that balances exposure, signalling, and repair. Similar caution applies to inflammation: chronic inflammation is damaging, but immune activation is also essential for infection, healing, and training adaptation.

Part two moves from cells to populations. Greger examines Mediterranean and Okinawan diets, several Blue Zones, centenarians, beverages, exercise, weight, sleep, stress, and social relationships. The repeated pattern is a diet rich in minimally processed plants, ordinary physical activity, little smoking, and sustained social structure.

The dependable dietary signal is broad. Whole grains, legumes, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds provide fibre, micronutrients, unsaturated fats, and diverse plant compounds while displacing sugary drinks, refined foods, processed meat, and excess salt. Healthy plant-based diet indices associate with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, although much of that evidence is observational.

Greger interprets the pattern as an argument for fully plant-based eating. The evidence more securely supports plant predominance and food quality than total exclusion. Mediterranean and other healthy patterns can include fish and small amounts of other animal foods. Reviews associate fish with several favourable outcomes, including all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. This does not make fish necessary or erase environmental and preparation questions. It means the book's strict conclusion is stronger than the comparative evidence.

Plant-based also needs a quality adjective. Chips, sweets, refined bread, and sugary drinks can be vegan. A strict whole-food version can be nutritious, but vitamin B12 needs a reliable source and calcium, iodine, vitamin D, iron, zinc, and long-chain omega-three status may need planning or monitoring. Every diet can be inadequate; a moral label does not solve nutrition.

Centenarian evidence requires equal restraint. Long-lived communities may combine legumes, vegetables, movement, purpose, close relationships, and low smoking. They also differ in genes, climate, migration, healthcare, income, birth records, cohort history, and survival. Age validation can show that exceptional longevity is real without identifying the behaviour that caused it. Okinawan survival cannot be assigned to sweet potatoes while everything else changed alongside diet.

The book gives less proportional attention to movement than its importance deserves. Aerobic activity supports cardiovascular fitness and metabolic health. Resistance work directly protects muscle and strength. Balance reduces falls. Movement can improve mood, cognition, sleep, and independence. These are not merely possible lifespan effects inferred from association; they are functional outcomes a person can measure.

The difference between leisure exercise and hard occupational labour also matters. Associations between activity and longevity can be confounded by health and income, while repetitive work with low control and poor recovery is not equivalent to a chosen progressive session. None of this makes exercise optional. It means the most relevant outcomes are capacity, symptoms, strength, blood pressure, glucose control, falls, and quality of life, not a single disputed estimate of added years.

Part three is the largest section and often the most useful. It covers bones, bowel and bladder function, circulation, hair, hearing, hormones, immunity, joints, mind, muscles, sex, skin, teeth, vision, and dignity in death. This changes the meaning of ageing from a laboratory score to lived capability.

It also produces an uneven reference book. High-value basics sit beside small food trials and interventions that ultimately do not work. Hearing aids, resistance training, sun protection, vaccination, dental care, screening, rehabilitation, treatment, and social support share pages with berries, extracts, powders, and homemade products. A reader must not infer that each paragraph carries equal weight.

Function compounds across systems. Hearing loss can increase isolation. Tooth loss can narrow diet. Muscle weakness raises fall risk. Poor vision reduces movement. Continence problems can reduce social life. Cognitive impairment threatens independence. Protecting healthspan therefore includes ordinary clinical care, accessible environments, relationships, and material support, not only molecular ageing.

The bones chapter should be read beside the muscle chapter. Bone responds to loading, but adequate energy, calcium, vitamin D, hormones, medication, and fall prevention can matter. Losing weight or restricting food can lower mechanical loading and nutrient intake. A plant-based pattern can support bone health when planned; it does not make low calcium or vitamin D irrelevant. Fracture prevention is a system of bone strength, muscle, balance, vision, environment, and appropriate treatment.

The sensory chapters carry an overlooked lesson: compensation can preserve life even when biology cannot be reversed. Hearing aids do not make an inner ear young, and glasses do not stop chronological ageing, but both can protect communication, movement, safety, and independence. A healthspan strategy should value adaptation as highly as a biomarker. The same applies to mobility aids, dental work, continence treatment, and home changes.

The mind chapter is particularly long because dementia is a major fear. The sensible priorities include vascular health, activity, avoiding smoking, treating hearing loss and other risk factors, maintaining connection, and seeking assessment for changes. Specific foods may contribute within a dietary pattern, but no berry or supplement guarantees prevention.

The muscle chapter reveals the book's largest practical conflict. Greger is interested in reducing protein, particular amino acids, calories, IGF-1, and mTOR signalling because restriction extends lifespan in some organisms. But older humans face anabolic resistance, meaning muscle responds less strongly to the same protein and training stimulus. Frailty, falls, illness, and poor healing are immediate threats.

Geriatric nutrition groups commonly advise at least one to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily for healthy older adults, with higher ranges during exercise or illness. Individual needs vary, including in kidney disease, but that is very different from treating protein as a general pro-ageing toxin. The source and overall pattern matter, yet adequacy comes first.

Calorie restriction needs similar safeguards. A two-year human trial in younger non-obese adults reduced fat and improved some risk factors but also reduced fat-free mass. Increasing the percentage of lean mass while losing actual muscle does not mean a person became stronger. Calorie restriction can also affect bone, hunger, healing, and nutritional adequacy. Human lifespan extension remains unproven, and frail or underweight older adults are the wrong population for casual restriction.

Part four presents eight priorities: nuts, greens, berries, xenohormesis and microRNA manipulation, prebiotics and postbiotics, calorie restriction, protein restriction, and preservation of NAD+. The list looks uniform but is not. Nuts, greens, and berries are straightforward foods inside a healthy pattern. They are valuable without being magic or uniquely necessary.

The later categories depend more heavily on mechanisms. Xenohormesis proposes that compounds made by stressed plants trigger useful human stress responses. MicroRNAs may transmit regulatory signals. Prebiotics feed microbes and postbiotics are their products. NAD+ participates in cellular energy and declines with age. Each is scientifically interesting. None supplies a simple, proven consumer action equivalent to eating vegetables.

Greger's scepticism is strongest when he tests commercial anti-ageing products. He examines collagen, antioxidants, hormones, resveratrol, NAD+ boosters, and many supplements rather than assuming “natural” means effective. Whole foods usually provide useful nutrition with less risk than concentrated compounds.

Even here, food and supplement claims need direct tests. Spermidine appears in wheat germ, mushrooms, and tempeh and can activate autophagy in models. A twelve-month randomised supplement trial in older adults with subjective cognitive decline did not improve the primary memory outcome. That result does not prove spermidine-rich food lacks value. It does show why a mechanism cannot carry the conclusion alone.

The source of a claim also matters. Food and drug companies can bias research, but advocacy organisations and investigators have commitments too. Funding, selective outcomes, publication bias, weak comparators, tiny samples, and attractive subgroup findings can affect any preferred intervention. Greger is right to examine industry influence. A fair method must then examine plant-food studies, supplement studies, and his own interpretation with the same suspicion.

How Not to Age is impressive in scope and often honest about null findings. Its problem is editing and calibration. Six hundred-plus pages, hundreds of external links, repeated plant-based conclusions, molecular acronyms, and obscure failed interventions make it difficult to see priorities. A reader can finish with a larger list and a weaker plan.

This is why the function section works better than the grand anti-ageing claim. A question such as how to preserve hearing or muscle has an outcome that matters and a bounded group of options. “Slow ageing” can absorb any molecule, food, animal experiment, or association and never reach a decisive test. The more general the promise, the easier it becomes to count biological plausibility as success.

No intervention should be judged only by whether it moves a target in the preferred direction. A lower growth signal may look favourable for cancer risk while being unfavourable for maintaining muscle. Lower weight can improve metabolic risk while worsening frailty. Restriction can lower one biomarker while reducing pleasure, resilience, or adherence. Ageing is made of trade-offs, so net function matters more than a collection of optimised parts.

Use it selectively. Begin with the function or claim that matters. Identify whether the evidence comes from cells, animals, biomarkers, cohorts, or randomised human outcomes. Compare the proposed food or supplement with activity, clinical care, and a simpler dietary pattern. Check absolute benefit, replication, feasibility, cost, and harm. Do not attempt every recommendation.

The reliable plan is compact. Do not smoke. Eat a varied diet built mostly from minimally processed plants. Move regularly, including aerobic, strength, and balance work. Protect adequate protein, energy, and micronutrients. Sleep, maintain relationships, and address stress. Use hearing, vision, dental, preventive, rehabilitative, and medical care when needed. Treat supplements as targeted tools, not proof that a pathway has been mastered.

The book's lasting meaning is not that ageing can be controlled with the correct shopping list. It is that biological decline has modifiable parts, function deserves active protection, and no expensive anti-ageing promise should outrank fundamentals. The reader's job is to preserve capability, not collect mechanisms.
