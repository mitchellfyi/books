Don't Believe Everything You Think is Joseph Nguyen's short guide to overthinking. Its central distinction is between thoughts and thinking. A thought arrives by itself: a memory, prediction, image or sentence. Thinking is the extra work that follows when you judge it, believe it, argue with it and build a larger story around it. You may not control the first thought, Nguyen says, but you do not have to keep feeding it.

Picture an argument that ended hours ago. The event is over, but the mind holds another trial: what you should have said, what the other person must think, what this proves about you. Nguyen calls that second layer thinking. His practical message is that a thought is not an instruction or a fact. If you notice it without joining it, the spiral can lose energy.

He builds a larger theory from this observation. We do not experience circumstances directly, he argues; we experience our thoughts about them. The same event affects people differently, and an old memory can stop hurting even though the past has not changed. Therefore, he concludes, psychological suffering comes from thinking rather than events. Beneath mental noise sits a natural state of peace, love and intuition. Flow, creative insight and sudden clarity appear when self-commentary becomes quiet.

The useful part of this claim is modest. Interpretation and rumination clearly shape experience. The overreach is the word all. Grief, trauma, bodily reactions, illness and material danger cannot be reduced to optional commentary. A first reaction may arrive already charged with fear or pain. The book works better as a prompt for noticing rumination than as a complete account of emotion.

Nguyen also divides goals by their emotional fuel. A goal driven by desperation tries to repair a feeling of inadequacy: achieve this, then I will be enough. Because that sense of lack travels with you, the milestone may feel strangely empty. A goal driven by inspiration grows from curiosity, interest or love of the work. The practical test is simple: ask what feeling is underneath the plan, and whether the goal expresses something you value or is being asked to rescue your identity.

The expanded edition adds more help than the original: practical steps, contemplative exercises, journal prompts, inner and outer work, and advice for common obstacles. A usable version is to pause when a spiral begins, write down what happened, then write what your mind has added. Allow the feeling to be present. Decide whether the situation requires action, acceptance or simply less rehearsal. This is thought-distancing, not forced positive thinking.

The distinction also changes how you respond to unwanted thoughts. Fighting a thought gives it attention and turns its presence into another problem. Nguyen recommends allowing it to pass without self-judgment. The aim is not a permanently blank mind. It is less attachment: you can experience doubt without making doubt the person in charge.

You still plan, decide and respond to real problems; you simply stop mistaking repeated worry for useful preparation.

That move resembles cognitive defusion in acceptance and commitment therapy: seeing thoughts as mental events instead of commands. It also has relatives in mindfulness and older contemplative traditions. Those similarities make the exercise plausible, but they do not prove Nguyen's broader spiritual theory. Evidence for a complete therapy cannot be transferred to every claim in a brief self-help book.

The reading experience is calm, direct and deliberately repetitive. Supporters value the absence of jargon and say the distinction helped interrupt rumination. Critics find the book padded, spiritually certain and short on evidence and limits. Both reactions make sense: repetition can turn an idea into a practice, but it does not add much information.

The content rates three point nine out of ten. It earns credit for clarity, accessibility and one useful exercise. It loses more for weak evidence, universal claims and an incomplete treatment of difficult emotions. Read the full book if gentle repetition helps ideas settle, or choose the expanded edition if you want its journal prompts. Skip it if you already practise thought-distancing, need deep theory, or want treatment-grade guidance. The five-minute version contains the main intellectual value: notice the thought, separate it from the story, and keep the freedom to act where action is actually needed.
