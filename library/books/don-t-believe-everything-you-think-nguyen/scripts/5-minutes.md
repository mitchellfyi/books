In March 2022, a young writer named Joseph Nguyen self-published a very short book with no publisher, no publicity, and no credentials — just the record of what had dissolved his own overthinking. He read passages of it over TikTok videos, word spread, and by the time the traditional industry noticed, Don't Believe Everything You Think had reached the New York Times bestseller list, more than thirty languages, and two million copies. It is roughly a hundred pages, readable in an evening, and it makes exactly one claim. Here is the claim, the case for it, and the professional verdict — which the book itself will not give you.

The claim: the root cause of all psychological suffering is not what happens to you. It is not even the thoughts that arise about what happens to you. It is thinking — the judging, elaborating, ruminating engagement you add on top. Thoughts, in Nguyen's telling, are like weather: spontaneous mental events that appear unbidden and pass on their own if left alone. Thinking is what you do with them — the fourth retelling of the argument, the courtroom you convene at two in the morning, the meaning you assign and then suffer from. You cannot stop thoughts, and you don't need to. You can stop believing them. The overthinker lying awake is not suffering from the day; they are suffering from their commentary on the day.

From this follows the book's engine, the inside-out claim: you never feel the world directly — you feel your thinking about it. The same event devastates one person and glances off another, so the feeling cannot live in the event. Recall something that once wrecked you and now doesn't: the event never changed; the thinking did. And beneath the noise, Nguyen says, there is a default state — peace, love, a quiet intuitive wisdom he attributes to the Universe and infinite intelligence — that you do not have to build, because it surfaces by itself whenever thinking settles. Flow states and shower clarity are his evidence: the mind working best precisely when self-commentary stops. The book's most practical chapter applies the frame to ambition: goals set from desperation — fear, lack, proving something — poison both the pursuit and the achievement, because the thinking travels with you; goals set from inspiration pull instead of push. The audit is one question: what feeling is underneath this plan? And the milestone that arrives feeling like nothing is the tell that the goal was set by the lack it was supposed to fix.

That is the whole book. The chapters restate it through new metaphors, each ending in summaries and reflection questions, and the repetition is either pedagogy or padding, depending on which of its sixty-eight thousand Goodreads reviewers you ask.

Now the verdict, because this is where a summary can outperform the original. What the book teaches has a clinical name: cognitive defusion — noticing thoughts as mental events rather than truths — and it is a real, evidence-based move at the heart of acceptance and commitment therapy. One professional reviewer maps the book onto ACT almost line by line: defusion, acceptance, present-moment awareness, simplified for lay readers, minus the rigour and the evidence base. The material is old — Stoic, Buddhist, and, closer to home, the unattributed echo of an inside-out psychology movement with its own documented evidence problems. The book cites none of this. And a practising therapist's detailed review lands two harder blows. First, the neat dichotomy misdescribes experience: real first reactions arrive already loaded — you do not think the neutral thought "I lost my job" and then add suffering; the impression arrives hurting, which is why the Stoics and modern therapy both start there. Second, the prescription omits the training: letting go of thoughts, he writes, is simple the way running a marathon is simple, and a book that sells the finish line without the training plan is — his words — radically incomplete but potentially misleading. There are no boundary conditions either: for grief, trauma, or clinical anxiety, "your thinking is the problem" shades into blaming the sufferer, and the book never marks the line.

Judged on content, it rates three and a half out of ten — weak: one useful move, inflated into a theory of everything, with no evidence, no sources, and no method. So take the move, which is real and free: next spiral, separate what happened from what your thinking has added since, and write both down. Run the desperation audit on one goal this week. And if the noticing helps you, go where it has a training literature — acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness-based CBT, or the Stoics. This book is a door, drawn on cardboard, to rooms that actually exist. Walk through the real ones.
