Every few years the book industry gets a lesson in how little it controls. In March 2022, Joseph Nguyen — a young writer with no publisher, no publicity budget, no platform to speak of, and no credentials in psychology — self-published a book barely a hundred pages long, written, he has said, not for a market but to give his own overthinking mind somewhere to rest. He read passages of it over TikTok videos, off camera, in a gentle voice. The algorithm did the rest. Don't Believe Everything You Think spread through online communities reader by reader until it reached the New York Times bestseller list, more than thirty languages, and over two million copies — after which the industry came to it: a new publishing company built around author partnerships repackaged it in an expanded hardcover edition for bookshops. It is one of the defining word-of-mouth phenomena of its publishing era — the proof text for BookTok's power to mint bestsellers without a single traditional gatekeeper, and the anchor acquisition for a publishing startup betting that the author, not the house, is now the brand. It is also — and an honest account has to hold both of these at once — a book whose entire content fits comfortably in the next few minutes, and whose sharpest professional review calls it radically incomplete but potentially misleading. Both facts are instructive, which is why this book earns a verdict unlike any other in this library: for once, the short version is not the compromise. It is the better version.

Start with the claim, because there is exactly one. Psychological suffering, Nguyen argues, has a single root cause. It is not your circumstances — not the job, the breakup, the bank balance. It is not even the thoughts that arise about your circumstances. It is thinking: the judging, elaborating, ruminating engagement you add on top of thoughts. The distinction between thoughts and thinking is the book's central machinery, so take it slowly. Thoughts, in Nguyen's account, are spontaneous mental events — like weather, they appear unbidden, uninvited, and morally neutral, and if left alone they pass on their own. You did not choose them; you cannot stop them; you are not responsible for them. Thinking is what you do with a thought: pick it up, turn it over, argue with it, build the case, convene the two-in-the-morning courtroom, run the fourth retelling of the argument you lost. Thoughts are free. Thinking is where the suffering is manufactured. The overthinker lying awake is not suffering from the day's events, which are over; they are suffering from their commentary on the day, which is live.

From the distinction flows the book's engine, what its unnamed tradition calls the inside-out understanding: you never feel the world directly. You feel your thinking about the world. Nothing can reach your emotional life except through the thought you have about it — which is why the same event devastates one person and glances off another, and why something that wrecked you five years ago can leave you indifferent today. The event never changed. The thinking did. Nguyen delivers this as self-evident, invites you to verify it in your own history, and builds everything on it: if feeling comes from thinking, then no improvement in circumstances can ever out-earn a noisy mind, and no circumstance can hurt a quiet one.

Beneath the noise, the book claims, there is a floor. Peace is not an achievement to be constructed but a default that surfaces on its own whenever thinking settles — along with love, contentment, and a quiet intuitive wisdom that Nguyen attributes, in the book's characteristic vocabulary, to the Universe and infinite intelligence. His evidence is experiential: the flow state, where athletes and artists perform best precisely because self-commentary has stopped; the shower insight, arriving exactly when you stopped working the problem; the strange calm that follows genuine letting-go. Creativity, in this picture, is not thought production but thought subtraction — wisdom arrives in the gaps. You do not build peace. You stop obscuring it.

The book's most practical chapter applies the frame to ambition, and it contains the one tool a reader can use the same afternoon. Goals, Nguyen says, come in two kinds distinguished not by content but by fuel. Goals of desperation are set from lack — fear, insufficiency, the need to prove something — and they poison both the pursuit and the achievement, because the lack travels with you: the business built to escape worthlessness delivers its milestone into the hands of someone still feeling worthless. Goals of inspiration are set from fullness — love of the work, curiosity, overflow — and they pull rather than push. The audit is a single question: what feeling is underneath this plan? And the diagnostic is one everyone recognises: the milestone that arrives feeling like nothing. That emptiness is the tell that the goal was set by the very lack it was supposed to repair.

That — genuinely — is the book. The remaining pages restate it through fresh metaphors, in very short chapters that end with bullet summaries and reflection questions, and the repetition is either the pedagogy or the padding depending on which of its sixty-eight thousand Goodreads reviewers you ask. The rating there is a lukewarm three-point-eight, split with unusual cleanness: five-star readers who say the book broke a rumination cycle nothing else had touched, and one-star readers who say the content fits in a few pages — a criticism made, notably, by people who agree with the content. The expanded edition adds material including a stepwise noticing exercise that the book's toughest professional reviewer singles out as its genuinely useful contribution — a small, teachable procedure in a book otherwise allergic to procedure — along with the endorsement economy of the modern bestseller, headlined by Simon Sinek's blurb calling it an essential first step to letting go of suffering. First step is, as we are about to see, exactly the right size of claim.

Now the verdict, and it deserves the same care as the claim, because this is what the book itself will never give you. What Nguyen teaches has a clinical name. Noticing thoughts as mental events rather than truths is called cognitive defusion, and it sits at the heart of acceptance and commitment therapy — ACT — one of the best-evidenced modern therapeutic frameworks. One professional reviewer maps the book onto ACT almost line by line: defusion, acceptance, present-moment awareness, helpfully simplified for lay readers, with the methodological rigour and the evidence base removed. Even the imagery is shared — thoughts as passing clouds, as leaves floating down a stream — metaphors that ACT clinicians assign as formal exercises and Nguyen deploys as reassurance. The lineage runs deeper: the Stoics built their entire practice on the handling of first impressions; Buddhist psychology has treated identification with thought as the engine of suffering for twenty-five centuries; and the book's specific inside-out vocabulary closely parallels a mid-century spiritual-psychology movement — the Three Principles — whose own evidence base is documented as thin and whose critics have called it a belief system dressed as science. Nguyen cites none of this. Not the therapy whose techniques he is teaching, not the traditions whose insights he is compressing, not the movement whose vocabulary he is echoing. The book presents twenty-five centuries of material as one man's discovery, and its authority is entirely autobiographical.

Does the unoriginality matter? Less than you might think — compression and warmth are real products, and for the book's core audience the absence of jargon is the feature. What matters more is the pair of blows landed by the book's most substantive professional review, from a practising therapist. First: the neat dichotomy misdescribes experience. Real first reactions do not arrive neutral. You do not think the flat, factual thought "I lost my job" and then, in a separable second step, add suffering to it. The impression arrives already hurting — the Stoics called it exactly that, an impression, and modern therapy calls it the activating event — which is why every serious tradition starts its training there, at the loaded arrival, not at an imaginary neutral thought. A book built on the neutral thought is built on something that, for the people who most need help, does not exist. Second: the missing method. The book prescribes letting go of thoughts as though it were a decision. Letting go of thoughts, the reviewer writes, is simple in the way that running a marathon is simple — the instruction fits in a sentence and the capacity takes years of training, which is what meditation traditions are for, and which this book never mentions. He invokes the old comedy sketch of the therapist whose entire treatment is shouting "stop it!" — and his verdict, worth quoting because it is the professional summary the book lacks, is that the result is radically incomplete but potentially misleading. Add the missing boundary conditions — the book never marks where its advice stops applying, and for grief, trauma, or clinical anxiety and depression, "your thinking is the root of your suffering" shades into blaming the sufferer — and you have the full critical picture.

Judged on content alone, as this library judges — the sales phenomenon and the celebrity endorsements excluded — Don't Believe Everything You Think rates three and a half out of ten: weak, the lowest score in this collection so far. The arithmetic is quick. The one distinction has genuine explanatory and practical value for newcomers, and the reception proves it reaches people the clinical literature does not. Everything else is dock: no evidence, no sources, no engagement with the fields whose material it teaches, universal unfalsifiable claims from a sample of one, no method, no boundary conditions, and — even at a hundred pages — a consensus among its own admirers that the idea fits in a few pages. Low density is forgivable at fifteen minutes; it is the reason a summary can carry the whole cargo.

One more reading of the phenomenon is worth having before the verdict, because it is the most charitable and probably the truest. Two million people did not buy a hundred-page book about thinking because they were fooled. They bought it because rumination is the background disease of the era and nobody had ever handed them the first tool. The book's success is less a measure of its argument than a census of unmet need — a generation of overthinkers meeting, in the plainest words anyone had used with them, the possibility that the commentary is optional. That is a real service, and the fact that better versions of the tool exist in books almost none of those readers would have opened is exactly the point. The cardboard door worked because it was the only door in the room.

So here is the decision, and for once it is simple. The short version genuinely suffices — that is the library's formal recommendation for this book, the first to earn it. Read the full book only in two cases: if you want the repetition itself, the hundred gentle pages functioning as a kind of guided sitting with one idea, which is a legitimate use and probably explains half its success; or if you need the least intimidating possible on-ramp to these ideas for yourself or someone else — a teenager, a parent allergic to therapy-speak — in which case its simplicity is precisely the point. Skip it in every other case, and especially if you have met these ideas anywhere before: any CBT workbook, any ACT book, any Stoicism primer, any mindfulness course contains this book plus the training it omits. And if your distress is clinical-grade, this is not the tool; it does not claim to be, but it also never says so, and that silence is its least forgivable omission.

Whatever you decide about the book, take the move — it is real, it is free, and it predates everyone who has ever sold it. Next time a spiral starts, separate the two layers on paper: what was the thought that arrived, and what has my thinking added since? Just seeing the two columns is half the technique. Run the fuel audit on one goal this week: inspiration or desperation underneath? And if the noticing genuinely helps you, honour that by going where the technique has a name, a literature, and a training plan — acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, or the Stoics, who got there first and left better instructions. This book is a door drawn on cardboard, pointing at rooms that actually exist. The kindest thing an honest account can do is hand you the real addresses.
