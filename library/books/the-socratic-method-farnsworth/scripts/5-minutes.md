Twenty-four centuries ago, a man made a career of asking questions so good that Athens executed him for it. The Socratic Method, published in 2021 by Ward Farnsworth, is a handbook for doing what that man did: not his conclusions, which he barely offered, but his procedure, which might be the most durable thinking tool ever invented.

Farnsworth is an unusual guide: a law professor and former dean of the University of Texas law school, who spent decades running the courtroom cousin of the method on students and wrote this book the way he wrote his earlier handbooks on rhetoric and Stoicism, by assembling hundreds of the best primary passages and walking you through them in plain English.

Here is the method he teaches. Socrates almost never argues that someone is wrong. He asks what they believe, gets it stated cleanly, and then finds something else they also believe that cannot sit beside the first claim. The refutation comes from inside the other person's own commitments. This move has a name, the elenchus, and it is why Socratic questioning teaches instead of merely defeating: nobody can dismiss a contradiction they themselves supplied. In dialogue after dialogue, someone confidently defines courage, or justice, or piety, and a short series of agreed examples quietly takes the definition apart.

Around the technique sits a discipline of humility that Farnsworth insists is not optional equipment. Socrates famously claimed to know only that he did not know, and the book presents that ignorance as the whole point of departure: the worst condition is not ignorance but ignorance that believes it knows. Many dialogues end in aporia, a puzzled dead end, and the book's most useful reframe is that this counts as progress, because puzzlement is what dissolved false certainty feels like from the inside. By the same logic, being refuted is winning. The person who shows you your error has handed you a gift no amount of agreement ever could.

That is why Farnsworth calls the method an ethic and not just a technique: patience, inquiry, humility, and doubt. The same questions asked with a different spirit become eristic, point-scoring, the courtroom cross-examination that shares Socrates' tools and inverts his purpose. The book is careful about this boundary, and it is what separates it from every debate-tactics manual on the shelf.

The later chapters follow the method to its descendants and to us. The Stoics, Farnsworth argues, took Socratic examination and turned it inward into a daily practice, which makes this book the foundation under the modern Stoicism revival his earlier book served. And the closing argument is about now: modern discourse, fast, tribal, certain, performed for audiences, is almost a precise inversion of the Socratic habit. Changed minds are treated as defeats. Questions are treated as attacks. The method decelerates reasoning, as the columnist George Will put it, making space for deliberation exactly where our habits close it down, an antidote to some social pandemics of our day.

The honest limits. This is a handbook, so scholarly controversies about Plato are smoothed over silently in favour of readability. The diagnosis of modern discourse is argued by illustration, not evidence. There are no drills, only worked examples, so readers hoping for a workbook find a guide instead. The middle chapters repeat the core moves by design, which patient readers experience as teaching and impatient ones as padding. And the method's failure modes, what happens against bad-faith opponents, or under power imbalances, get little attention.

What survives all of that is considerable. A learnable set of question-forms for testing any claim. A discipline for auditing your own beliefs, which is where the method matters most, since your own mind is the one interlocutor you can never walk away from. And a standard for what disagreement could be: collaborative, slow, and aimed at the truth rather than the scoreboard.

Read the full book if you want the moves shown in the original exchanges, patiently, with the ethic argued; it is short, beautifully organised, and rereadable. Stop here if you wanted the method's core, its spirit, and its caveats. Skip it if you came for debate ammunition, because it is aimed at the one arguer you can actually reform.

Try the method tonight. Take something you are sure of, and ask what else you believe that sits uneasily next to it. If the question stings a little, Socrates would say it is working.
