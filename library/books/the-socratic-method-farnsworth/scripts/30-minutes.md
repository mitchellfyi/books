Athens, three hundred and ninety-nine years before the common era. A seventy-year-old man stands trial before a jury of five hundred of his fellow citizens. He has no money to speak of, holds no office, commands no army. His crime, formally, is impiety and corrupting the young. His crime, actually, is questions. For decades he has wandered the city asking prominent men to explain the things they were certain of, justice, courage, piety, excellence, and demonstrating, gently and in public, that they could not. The jury votes to kill him. He accepts the verdict with the observation that he would rather die having examined life than live without examining it, drinks the hemlock, and becomes, by way of his student Plato's writings, the most influential thinker in the history of the West. Twenty-four centuries later, a law school dean in Texas sat down to write a practical manual for doing the exact thing Athens executed him for. That manual is The Socratic Method: A Practitioner's Handbook, published in 2021 by Ward Farnsworth, and its wager is simple: the procedure that got Socrates killed is learnable, portable, and more necessary right now than at any point since.

Understand the guide before the tour. Farnsworth is not a classicist; he is a lawyer, and a distinguished one: professor and then dean of the University of Texas School of Law for a decade, former clerk to two of the most formidable judges in modern American law, and a teacher who spent his career running the law-school variant of Socratic questioning on generations of students. He is also, in his parallel life, the finest modern anthologist of classical practical wisdom: his earlier handbooks did for English rhetoric and for Stoicism what this book does for Socrates, assembling hundreds of the best primary passages ever written on a subject, organising them by structure rather than chronology, and threading patient, plain-English commentary between them. The format matters, because it fixes what most books about thinking get wrong. Farnsworth barely asks you to take his word for anything. Every claim about what Socrates does is shown, in quoted dialogue, on the page. The book is a workshop with the original master demonstrating at the bench.

Begin, as the book does, with a correction that changes everything downstream. Socrates left no doctrine. He wrote nothing at all; what survives is Plato's dramatisations of him in conversation, and in those conversations he asserts almost nothing, confesses ignorance constantly, and reaches conclusions rarely. What he left instead is a procedure, a repeatable way of examining beliefs, and the procedure, not any set of answers, is the inheritance. This is what makes the method portable across twenty-four centuries. You can run Socratic questioning on the ethics of a business decision, on a political conviction, on your own certainty about a colleague, without adopting a single ancient opinion. The method is content-neutral, which is precisely why it frightens the confident of every era: it works on anything, including whatever you happen to be sure of today.

The core of the procedure has a Greek name the book teaches early: the elenchus, the consistency test. Attend to its mechanics, because they are stranger and cleverer than the popular image of refutation. Socrates does not marshal evidence against your claim. He does not cite authorities, produce statistics, or even, usually, say you are wrong. He asks what you believe, helps you state it cleanly, and then goes hunting, not in the world, but inside you, for something else you also believe that cannot live beside the first claim. The contradiction, when it springs, is entirely yours: both beliefs came out of your own mouth, minutes apart, freely. This is the move's genius as pedagogy. An argument from outside can be deflected forever; every debater knows a hundred ways. But a contradiction between your own commitments, freshly stated, in front of you, cannot be dismissed as someone else's opinion. It can only be resolved, by revising one belief or the other, or endured, in the uncomfortable knowledge that your set of beliefs does not cohere. Farnsworth generalises the point into the book's sharpest practical claim: your beliefs form a set, the set has almost certainly never been audited for internal consistency, and until it has, you are living by contradictions you cannot see. The elenchus is the audit.

Watch the audit run once, in the pattern the dialogues repeat most famously. A general defines courage: standing firm and never retreating. Question one: courage is a fine and admirable thing? Of course. Question two: a commander who retreats to higher ground, saving his army to win tomorrow, acts wisely? Certainly. Question three: can wise conduct in battle be cowardice? Surely not. Then the retreating commander was courageous, and courage cannot be never retreating. Three easy agreements, no evidence introduced, no voice raised, and the definition has fallen down entirely on its own. The general is not defeated; he is enlightened, or at least he could be, which brings us to the fork in the road that the whole book guards.

Because those same three questions can be two completely different acts. Done as combat, they are a trap: the questioner knew the destination, walked the victim into it, and enjoys the audience's laughter. The Greeks had a word for this too, eristic, argument for victory, and its modern descendants are everywhere, the gotcha interview, the debate-club kill, the quote-tweet dunk. Done Socratically, the same questions are a collaboration: two people jointly discovering that courage is more interesting than either of them thought, with the questioner genuinely ready to be the one refuted. Same tools, opposite purposes, and the difference is not in the technique but in the character running it. This is why Farnsworth insists, throughout the book, that the method is an ethic before it is a technique: patience, because the small steps cannot be rushed; inquiry, because you must actually want the answer; humility, because your own beliefs must face the same blade; and doubt, held open on principle, because certainty is the disease this whole practice treats. Strip the ethic and keep the moves, and you have not learned the Socratic method. You have learned cross-examination, which is the method's evil twin, and Farnsworth, who taught in the profession that perfected the twin, draws the boundary with an insider's precision.

The second great component of the method is its strange starting point: ignorance, properly understood. Socrates' most famous utterance, that he knows only that he does not know, is usually quoted as charming modesty. The book restores it as diagnosis. The human default, in Athens and on the internet, is double ignorance: not knowing, while believing that you know. Single ignorance is innocent and curable; double ignorance is confident, articulate, runs governments, and cannot be cured until it is first revealed, which is what the elenchus is for. And this reframing rescues the dialogues' most puzzling feature, the fact that so many of them end without an answer, in the state the Greeks called aporia, puzzlement, the path exhausted. Generations of readers have found these endings unsatisfying, and Farnsworth's response is the book's most useful single reframe: aporia is not the method failing, it is the method succeeding. Puzzlement is what dissolved false certainty feels like from the inside. The general walked in knowing what courage was and walks out genuinely unsure, and his unsureness is truer than his knowledge was. On the same inversion, being refuted is winning. The person who shows you a contradiction in your beliefs has handed you a fragment of the map showing exactly where your understanding fails, something no ally, flatterer, or algorithmic feed will ever give you. The emotional reflex to defend, to experience refutation as attack, is, in the book's account, the single largest obstacle between ordinary minds and good thinking, and the method is, among other things, a training regime for dismantling that reflex.

The middle of the book is the craft school, and it earns the handbook of the title. Farnsworth breaks the questioning into learnable forms and annotates them from the dialogues the way a chess book annotates grandmaster games. How to elicit a claim cleanly, because vague beliefs are untestable and most confident opinion is vapour until it is phrased; the first Socratic service is often just forcing precision. How to choose test cases: begin with the ordinary example that everyone accepts, then move to the edge case where the stated principle starts to bend, the retreat that is wise, the lie that is kind, the theft that saves a life. How to build agreement in small, almost boring steps, each one individually undeniable, so that the conclusion arrives self-assembled from the other person's own materials. How to offer the counterexample as a question rather than a verdict, which keeps the inquiry joint and the defences down. None of these forms is exotic; all of them are learnable; and the annotated reconstructions teach something no summary can, the feel of the sequence, the patience of it, the way the crucial question is always smaller and earlier than you expect. You finish these chapters seeing the skeleton under every serious conversation you have ever had, and wincing at the ones you conducted by assertion.

The book then follows the method downstream, first to its most successful franchise. The Stoics, the subject of Farnsworth's previous handbook, took the Socratic examination of belief and did two things to it: turned it inward, and made it daily. Where Socrates audited the beliefs of others in the marketplace, Epictetus and his heirs audited their own, every day, as maintenance: is this judgement true? Is this within my control? What do I actually believe here, and does it cohere? Modern readers who arrived at Stoicism through its recent revival will find this chapter quietly reorienting: the daily practices they know are applied Socratism, the elenchus run on oneself, and this book turns out to be the missing foundation under that entire shelf. For self-examination is where the method matters most and performs best. Your own mind is the one interlocutor who can never storm off, never play to the crowd, and never quite get away with the evasions that work in public.

And then the book arrives at us, and at the argument that made a quiet philosophy manual a minor cultural event. Modern discourse, Farnsworth argues, is not merely bad at the Socratic virtues; it is their systematic inversion, almost as if designed to be. The method is slow; the feed is instant. The method doubts; the feed rewards certainty. The method follows the argument wherever it goes; the feed follows the tribe. The method addresses one mind in good faith; the feed performs for an audience and scores by applause. The method treats a changed mind as the entire point of the exercise; the feed treats it as humiliation, apostasy, weakness. Assertion is cheap, contempt is content, and the phrase doing your own research has come to mean its own opposite. Into this, Farnsworth offers not a policy but a secession: you cannot fix the discourse, but you can withdraw your own mind from its habits, one conversation and one self-audit at a time. The columnist George Will, giving the book a column, compressed the case into a sentence the publisher rightly kept: the method decelerates reasoning, making space for deliberation where disagreements arise, an antidote to some social pandemics of our day. The book's own version is smaller and sturdier: the quality of your thinking is set by the quality of the questions you are willing to sit inside, and that, unlike the discourse, is entirely in your control.

What is it actually like to read? Short, first of all: around two hundred and sixty pages, organised with a lawyer's tidiness into chapters that each teach one element, state it plainly, demonstrate it in quotation, and stop. The voice is a teacher's, patient, lucid, dryly funny, and entirely without evangelism; Farnsworth persuades the way his subject does, by walking you there in small agreed steps. The quotations are generous and constant, and they are the book's spine: it is as much a curated anthology of the best Socratic passages as a commentary on them, which means you spend the book in contact with the original voices rather than with paraphrase. It reads well in sittings, rereads even better, and sits naturally beside a copy of the dialogues as a field guide. The costs of the format are real and worth naming. The core moves recur across chapters by design, and readers moving fast experience the recurrence as repetition rather than reinforcement. The word handbook sets some readers up to expect exercises, drills, a course; what they get is worked examples and the instruction to go practise, which is not quite the same. And the scholarship is deliberately smoothed: the genuine controversies, how much of this Socrates is Plato's puppet, whether early and late dialogues show different methods, whether the method survives separation from the metaphysics, are settled silently in favour of a readable line. It is a practitioner's book built on scholarship, not a contribution to the debates, and honest about it.

How good is the evidence? For once in this genre, genuinely strong, because of what the claims are. The book claims that the method consists of certain moves and a certain spirit, and it demonstrates every such claim in quoted primary text you can check against the cited dialogue. The reconstructions are transparent; the author's classroom decades ground the practical judgements; and the reasoning is shown, not asserted. The soft spots are at the edges. The modern-discourse diagnosis, however recognisable, is argued by illustration rather than evidence, and a sceptic could ask for the data. The method's failure modes get almost no treatment, and they matter: run against a bad-faith opponent, Socratic questioning becomes material for their performance; run upward against power, it reads as insubordination; run at the wrong moment, it is insufferable, and the man who invented it was, let us remember, executed by the citizens he questioned. And there is no engagement with contemporary research on whether Socratic pedagogy actually outperforms alternatives, a question on which evidence exists. The book teaches the instrument superbly. Where, when, and on whom to play it remains entirely your judgement, and the book's silence there is its largest gap.

Reception matched the book's temperament: quietly excellent, across audiences that rarely agree. Philosophers praised it without condescension; Rebecca Goldstein called it elegant and erudite, its virtues worn lightly. A syndicated conservative columnist devoted a column to it. The Stoicism community adopted it as their foundation text. Lawyers and teachers recognised their daily craft finally written down with its ethics attached. Reader ratings run warm, and the recurring complaints are mild and structural, some repetition, fewer drills than hoped, rather than substantive; almost no one disputes the content, which is what happens when claims are demonstrated from primary text. If this library's shelves are your context, one contrast is illuminating: The 48 Laws of Power, elsewhere in this collection, treats conversation as position warfare, information as ammunition, and the changed mind as defeat. Farnsworth's book is its exact philosophical negative: argument as joint inquiry, information as gift, the changed mind as the entire victory. Read consecutively, they are a complete education in what a question can be used for.

Be precise about what this book is not, so it disappoints nobody. It is not a biography of Socrates, a history of Greek philosophy, or a survey of the trial; the drama stays offstage. It is not new Plato scholarship and does not adjudicate academic disputes. It is not a debate manual; if you arrive wanting to win arguments, the book will spend two hundred pages quietly redirecting the weapon toward your own certainties. It is not a structured course; there are no exercises, only examples and the standing instruction to practise. And it is not a quick read in effect, whatever its length, because its actual use is as a practice you either adopt or shelve.

The decision, then. Read the full book if you want the method properly: the moves annotated inside the original exchanges, the ethic argued, the sources assembled into a permanent reference; it is the best single entry point to Socratic practice in print, and one of the rare thinking-skills books whose evidence is checkable on its own pages. Stop at this length if you came for the core, the elenchus, the discipline of ignorance, the ethic that separates inquiry from combat, and the case for its urgency; you now have all of it. Skip it if you wanted ammunition, scholarship, or a workbook, all three of which it deliberately declines to be. Print suits it best; the block quotations reward a pencil, and audio flattens the dialogue structure the book is built from.

And run the experiment before the feeling fades, because the method only exists in use. Choose one belief you hold with genuine confidence, about your work, your politics, a person you have judged. State it cleanly, one sentence, no hedges. Now go looking, honestly, patiently, for the other things you also believe that sit uneasily beside it, and ask the small questions in order. Do not defend; just look. If somewhere in the sequence you feel the floor shift slightly under your certainty, hold still in that feeling for a moment instead of scrambling back to solid ground. The Greeks had a word for where you are standing. It is twenty-four centuries old, a free man died for the right to put you there, and according to this patient, elegant book, it is the place where thinking actually begins.
