In Plato's dialogues there is a scene that repeats so often it becomes a rhythm. A confident man, a general, a priest, a famous teacher, tells Socrates exactly what courage is, or piety, or justice. Socrates seems delighted. He asks a small question. The confident man agrees to something obvious. Another small question, another easy agreement. And then, three or four steps later, the confident man discovers that the obvious things he just agreed to have quietly demolished the definition he began with. He knew, until he was asked. That scene, and the machinery inside it, is the subject of The Socratic Method: A Practitioner's Handbook, published in 2021 by Ward Farnsworth, a book with the unusual ambition of teaching you to do the demolition yourself, starting with your own opinions.

Farnsworth is a curious and fitting guide. He is not a classicist but a lawyer: a professor and, for a decade, dean of the University of Texas law school, and before that a clerk to two of America's most formidable judges. Law school famously runs on its own harsher cousin of the Socratic method, and Farnsworth spent decades wielding it before writing this book. He is also, on the side, one of the great modern anthologists of classical wisdom: his earlier handbooks assembled the best of English rhetoric and the core of Stoic philosophy the same way this one works, hundreds of primary passages, organised by structure rather than history, with patient plain-English commentary in between. The result reads less like a book about Socrates than a workshop manual with Socrates as the demonstration.

Start where the book starts, with a correction. Socrates left no doctrine. He wrote nothing; what he left, through Plato's dramatisations, is a procedure, and the procedure, not any conclusion, is the inheritance. This matters because it makes the method portable. You can run Socratic questioning on ethics, on business strategy, on your own political certainties, without buying any ancient metaphysics. The book's premise is that this procedure is a craft, learnable like any craft, from worked examples, and that most of what passes for thinking, then and now, is the recitation of unexamined opinion, what the book calls the real target of Socrates' entire career.

The core move has a name: the elenchus. Watch it carefully, because it is not what most people imagine refutation to be. Socrates almost never tells anyone they are wrong, and he almost never argues against a claim with evidence of his own. Instead he elicits. What exactly do you believe? Stated how? And then he goes looking, not in the world but inside the same person, for something else they also believe that cannot live beside the first claim. The general who defines courage as standing firm in battle also believes a tactical retreat can be courageous. The contradiction is not Socrates' opinion versus yours; it is you versus you. That is what gives the method its strange power to teach rather than merely to win. A person can dismiss your counterargument forever; they cannot easily dismiss their own beliefs, freshly stated, sitting in plain contradiction. Consistency-testing, the book argues, is the sharpest all-purpose blade in the reasoning drawer, and the reader is meant to take it home: your beliefs form a set, and until they have been tested against each other, the set almost certainly contains contradictions you are living by.

The method's second component is a discipline of ignorance, and Farnsworth is at his best explaining why this is not modesty theatre. Socrates' famous claim, that he knows only that he does not know, defines the condition the method exists to treat: double ignorance, not knowing while believing that you know. The confident interlocutors are not stupid; they are unexamined. And the dialogues' notorious habit of ending without an answer, in a state the Greeks called aporia, puzzlement, is reframed in this book as the method succeeding. Puzzlement is what dissolved false certainty feels like from the inside. You walked in knowing what justice is; you walk out genuinely unsure, and that unsureness is closer to the truth than your confidence was. By the same inversion, being refuted is winning. The person who shows you that your beliefs contradict each other has given you something no supporter ever gives you: a map fragment showing exactly where your understanding fails. The emotional reflex to defend, Farnsworth argues, is the single biggest obstacle to thinking well, and the method is, among other things, a training regime against that reflex.

Which is why the book keeps insisting, chapter after chapter, that the method is an ethic and not merely a technique. The moves without the character have a different ancient name, eristic: argument as combat, questioning as trap-setting, the win valued over the truth. Farnsworth draws the line sharply, and he should know where it runs, because courtroom cross-examination lives on the far side of it, sharing every tool with Socrates and inverting the purpose. What makes questioning Socratic is patience, genuine inquiry, humility about your own side, and doubt held open on principle. You must want the truth more than the point. You must be as severe with your own beliefs as with your opponent's, ideally more. Run with that spirit, the method is collaborative, two people jointly auditing a belief neither fully trusts. Run without it, the same questions are just rhetoric with a philosophy degree.

To feel the difference the spirit makes, run the method's most famous pattern once, slowly. Someone defines courage as standing firm and never retreating. Question one: is courage a fine and admirable thing? Obviously. Question two: can a commander who retreats to higher ground, saving his army to win tomorrow, be acting wisely? Agreed. Question three: can acting wisely in battle be cowardly? Surely not. Then the man who retreated was courageous, and courage cannot be never retreating. Notice what happened: no evidence was introduced, no authority cited, nobody called anyone wrong. Three easy agreements, each one freely given, and the definition fell down entirely on its own. Now notice the two ways that moment can land. Done eristically, it is a humiliation with an audience. Done Socratically, it is two people discovering together that courage is more interesting than either of them thought. Same moves. Different ethic. The whole book lives in that difference.

The craft chapters, the heart of the handbook, break the questioning into learnable forms. How Socrates gets a claim stated cleanly enough to test, because vague beliefs are unfalsifiable and most confident opinions are vague. How he chooses test cases that isolate a principle, ordinary examples first, then the edge case where the principle bends. How agreement is built one small, almost boring step at a time, so that the conclusion, when it arrives, is self-inflicted, assembled entirely from things the other person said. How counterexamples are offered as questions rather than verdicts. Farnsworth reconstructs passages from the dialogues and annotates them move by move, the way a chess book annotates games, and the effect is demystifying in the best way: you finish the chapters able to see the skeleton under conversations you have been having badly your whole life.

The last movement of the book runs the method forward, first to its descendants, then to us. The Stoics, subject of Farnsworth's previous handbook, are presented as the method's most successful franchise: they took the Socratic examination of belief and turned it inward, made it daily, a maintenance practice for one's own judgements. If you have read modern Stoicism, this chapter quietly reveals it as applied Socratism. And then the contemporary argument, the reason this quiet book found an audience. Modern discourse, Farnsworth argues, is a near-perfect inversion of everything the method trains. It is fast where the method is slow; certain where the method doubts; tribal where the method follows the argument; performed for an audience where the method addresses one mind at a time. Social media rewards assertion, punishes the changed mind as weakness, and makes contempt the cheapest content there is. The columnist George Will, reviewing the book, compressed its cultural case into a line: the method decelerates reasoning, making space for deliberation where disagreement lives, an antidote to some social pandemics of our day. Farnsworth's version is more modest and more practical: you will not fix the discourse, but you can secede from its habits, one conversation, and one self-examination, at a time.

A word about the law-school connection, since the phrase Socratic method probably reached you through a legal drama. The cold-calling interrogation of first-year law students is the method's best-known modern descendant, and Farnsworth ran it for decades, which gives his account an insider's precision about what the classroom version keeps and what it loses. It keeps the structure: claims elicited, hypotheticals posed, principles stress-tested against edge cases until they bend. What it often loses is the ethic, when the exercise becomes performance under pressure rather than joint inquiry. The book quietly reads as the correction of its own profession: this is what the questioning is for, written by someone who has watched it used for other things.

What is the book like to hold and read? Short, at around two hundred and sixty pages, and organised with a lawyer's tidiness: each chapter teaches one element, states it plainly, shows it in quoted dialogue, and moves on. The voice is patient, lucid, and quietly funny, a teacher's voice with no evangelism in it. The quotations are generous and they are the point: claims about what Socrates does are shown, in his own staged words, rather than asserted. It reads as well in sittings as straight through, and it is built to be reread. The honest costs of the format: the core moves recur across chapters by design, which reads as patient reinforcement or as repetition depending on your speed; readers who took the word handbook to promise exercises and drills find worked examples instead; and the genuine scholarly controversies, how much of this Socrates is Plato's invention, whether the method is separable from the philosophy, are resolved silently in favour of a smooth read. This is a practitioner's book resting on scholarship, not a contribution to it, and it never pretends otherwise.

The reception matched the book's temperament: quietly excellent. Philosophers praised it without condescension, Rebecca Goldstein called it elegant and erudite with its virtues lightly worn. A conservative columnist gave it one of his columns; Stoicism readers adopted it as the missing foundation under their practice; lawyers and teachers recognised their own craft finally written down properly. Reader ratings run warm, and the complaints are gentle and consistent: some repetition, fewer exercises than hoped. Almost nobody disputes the content itself, which is what happens when a book's claims are demonstrated from primary text rather than argued from authority.

For balance, hold on to what the book does not do. It offers no account of the method's failure modes, and they are real: Socratic questioning against a bad-faith opponent becomes material for their performance; run upward against power it can read as insubordination; and Socrates' own fate, a death sentence from his fellow citizens, is a standing reminder that relentless examination is not always received as a gift. It does not engage modern pedagogy research on whether Socratic teaching works. And its diagnosis of contemporary discourse, however plausible, is illustrated rather than evidenced. The book teaches the instrument beautifully; where and when to play it remains your judgement.

So the decision. Read the full book if you want the method properly: the moves shown inside the original exchanges, annotated patiently, with the ethic argued and the sources assembled; it is short, rereadable, and the best single entry point to Socratic practice in print. Stop at this length if you wanted the core, the elenchus, the discipline of ignorance, the ethic, and the reasons it matters now; you have them, and tonight's experiment requires nothing more. Skip it if you were hoping for debate ammunition or a workbook, because it will disappoint you twice, first by refusing to arm you against others, then by aiming everything at you.

And take the experiment. Choose one belief you hold with real confidence, about your work, your politics, a person. Now go looking, honestly, for the other things you believe that sit uneasily beside it. Ask the small, easy questions in order. If somewhere in the sequence you feel the floor shift slightly, that sensation has a Greek name, it is twenty-four centuries old, and according to this book it is the feeling of thinking finally being done properly.
