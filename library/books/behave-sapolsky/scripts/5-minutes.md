A finger tightens on a trigger. Or a hand reaches out to a stranger. Why? Robert Sapolsky — Stanford neuroscientist, MacArthur Fellow, and thirty-year veteran of both laboratory science and Kenyan baboon fieldwork — spent a career on that question, and Behave, published in 2017, is his eight-hundred-page answer. Its structure is the answer: rewind the clock. What happened in the brain one second before the act — which neurons, the amygdala or the frontal cortex? What sights and smells primed them in the minutes before? What hormones set the sensitivities in the hours and days before? What did adolescence, childhood, and the womb build years before? What did culture write over centuries, and evolution over millions of years? The book's founding claim is that every one of those answers is true at once, and that picking one — a gene for this, a brain region for that — is not simplification but error. Nature versus nurture is not a debate the book settles; it is a question the book retires.

Run the machine and the famous headline biology falls apart productively. Testosterone does not cause aggression: it amplifies whatever behaviour the context already rewards — in one economic game, testosterone made players more generous, because generosity was the status move on offer. Oxytocin is not the love hormone: it deepens bonding with Us while sharpening hostility toward Them. And there is no gene "for" anything interesting: genes are if-then clauses whose meaning depends on environment, which is why heritability numbers collapse when contexts change. The book's refrain — it's complicated, it depends — is not a failure to conclude. It is the conclusion, earned mechanism by mechanism.

The applications are where the book bites. Us versus Them: the brain sorts faces into in-group and out-group in fractions of a second, before awareness — the fastest, most consequential categorisation we make, and the root of our worst behaviour. The redemptive half of the finding is that the categories are arbitrary and cheap to redraw: a shared team cap, a cross-cutting identity, genuine contact — and the biology follows. The adolescent brain: dopamine systems mature early and roar; the frontal cortex, the restraint hardware, is not fully wired until the mid-twenties — which explains adolescent risk, tribalism, and idealism as a timetable rather than a defect, and quietly indicts how justice systems treat the young. And running through everything, the thesis in the title: our best and our worst use the same machinery. The oxytocin that bonds mothers bonds militias. The conformity that carries genocide carries barn-raisings. The book refuses a biology of villains — which is bleak about monsters and hopeful about levers, because if context configures the machinery, contexts can be built.

The last chapters draw the hard consequence: if every cause traces backward — neurons to hormones to childhood to genes to culture — the space where uncaused free choice could hide keeps shrinking. Sapolsky's provocation, completed six years later in his book Determined, is that nothing is left, and that blame and praise are biologically incoherent even where restraint remains necessary. Philosophers, including sympathetic ones, find that argument undercooked — compatibilism is dismissed rather than engaged — and it remains the book's most contested chapter.

The honest ledger. Behave is the best-evidenced book in its genre by a distance: named studies, counter-studies engaged, two appendices that actually teach the neuroscience. The reception was a near-unanimous rave — "magisterial" appears in the New York Times, the Wall Street Journal, the Guardian, Nature, and Science. The documented critique, from a physician-scientist in Skeptical Inquirer, lands real blows anyway: the book never grapples with the replication crisis that hit exactly its 2017 social-psychology sources; brain-imaging findings are stated more confidently than fMRI's blood-flow measurements warrant; effect sizes go underreported; and specific claims — substance P blockers for depression, adult neurogenesis — are disputed or wrong. Add the universal note that eight hundred dense pages are a real toll, and the content rates six point three out of ten: worthwhile, the evidentiary summit of its shelf.

So: read the full book if you want the machinery itself — it is a course, the appendices are genuinely teachable, and finishing it upgrades every behavioural claim you meet afterwards. Stop here if you wanted the architecture: the backwards clock, context-not-cause, Us/Them, the adolescent cortex, and the caveats. Either way, take the frame home tonight: pick one behaviour you condemned this week and explain it at every timescale — the second, the hour, the decade, the century — before you re-judge it. That exercise is the book in miniature, and once you have run it honestly, single-cause stories about human beings never quite convince you again.
