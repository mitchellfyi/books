Somewhere around seventy thousand years ago, an unremarkable ape in a corner of Africa picked up a strange new ability: it could talk about things that do not exist. Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens — a Hebrew University lecture course that became one of the defining books of the 2010s, sixty-five languages, years on the bestseller lists — is the story of what that trick did, organised around one idea sharp enough to reorganise how you see your own life.

The idea is this: humans dominate the planet not because we are strong or even individually clever, but because we alone cooperate flexibly in unlimited numbers — and the mechanism is fiction. A chimpanzee can trust the troop members it grooms; cooperation caps at acquaintance. But strangers who believe the same story — a god, a nation, a currency — can trust each other without ever meeting. Money, laws, corporations, and human rights exist nowhere in physics or biology; they are what Harari calls intersubjective realities, real only because enough minds agree they are. His set piece is Peugeot: a company that owns factories and signs contracts yet exists only in collective legal imagination — it would survive the destruction of every car and the death of every employee. Once you see the frame, institutions stop looking natural and start looking maintained. Money is simply the most successful fiction of all: the one story everyone believes, which is why a banknote works between people who share no god, no ruler, and no language.

The book runs this idea through four revolutions. The Cognitive Revolution gave sapiens fictive language, and shortly afterward — the book keeps this uncomfortable fact front and centre — every other human species on Earth disappeared. There were at least six; only the storyteller survived. The Agricultural Revolution, ten thousand years ago, Harari calls history's biggest fraud: farming multiplied the species while making the average member's life worse — monotonous food, epidemics, hierarchy, longer hours — and population growth foreclosed any way back. His famous inversion: wheat domesticated us. The engine is what he calls the luxury trap: improvements that become necessities and then chains, a pattern that runs unbroken from grain to email. The Unification of Humankind followed, as three universal orders — money, empire, and religion — knitted thousands of separate cultures into one world. And the Scientific Revolution began, in his telling, when Europeans did something new: admitted ignorance, then married the resulting science to empire and capital in a feedback loop of knowledge, conquest, and credit that built modernity.

Two more moves give the book its conscience. First, the audit: none of this demonstrably made anyone happier. Happiness tracks expectations and biochemistry more than conditions, so centuries of progress may have raised the bar rather than the mood — and the one population whose lot unambiguously worsened is the animals in industrial farms, which Harari bluntly ranks among history's greatest crimes. Second, the ending: sapiens is acquiring the powers of intelligent design — genetic engineering, brain-machine interfaces — and the book closes on its most quoted warning: we are self-made gods who do not know what we want.

Now the reliability report, because this book earned an unusually specific one. A philosopher reviewing it found "carelessness, exaggeration and sensationalism." A science journalist smelled "dorm-room bull sessions" in its unsourced assertions. A career anthropologist concluded that where Harari is right he is unoriginal, and where he is original he is often wrong. And an evolutionary biologist published a genuine error catalogue: animal communication misdescribed as language, chimpanzees fighting cheetahs that live on different parts of the continent, an Ecuadorian people cited as evidence for state pacification who have been peaceful without a state for fifty years. Harari's own doctoral supervisor offered the kindest cut: he asks questions so large that no one can say any particular bit is wrong. The frames survive this record; the facts should be checked before you repeat them. Judged on content, that mix rates five point four out of ten — limited: magnificent conceptual handles on a weak evidential chassis.

So: read the full book if you want the frames at full seductive force — it is the fastest-reading grand narrative in modern nonfiction, and its set pieces are genuinely brilliant. Stop here if you wanted the four revolutions, the fictions lens, and the honest calibration the book itself omits. And either way, keep its best habit and turn it on everything, including the book: pick any institution this week — your bank, your employer, your country — and ask who believes its story, and who enforces it. Then pick any confident synthesis and check one load-bearing fact. Harari taught a generation to do the first. His critics taught it to do the second. You need both.
