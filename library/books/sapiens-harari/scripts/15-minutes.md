For most of prehistory, being human was not special. At least six human species shared the planet — sturdy Neanderthals in Europe, tiny islanders in Indonesia, upright cousins across Asia — and ours was a middling ape in a corner of Africa, hiding from lions and cracking bones for marrow. Then, somewhere around seventy thousand years ago, Homo sapiens picked up a trick no animal had ever had, and within a few tens of thousands of years every other human species on Earth was gone. Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind — a Hebrew University lecture course that became a Hebrew bestseller in 2011, an English one in 2014, and eventually one of the defining books of its decade, sixty-five languages, years on the lists, waved around by presidents and tech founders — is the story of that trick and everything it built. It is also — and what follows treats both facts with equal seriousness — the era's test case for what happens when a book becomes too successful for anyone to check.

The trick, in Harari's telling, is fiction. Plenty of animals communicate; vervet monkeys can warn each other about eagles. Harari runs through the standard theories of why human language evolved and favours the least flattering: gossip. Language's first killer application was talking about each other — tracking who is reliable, who cheats, who is sleeping with whom — because in a social species, alliance information is survival information. But gossip only scales so far. The move that changed everything sits one step beyond it: only sapiens can describe things that do not exist — spirits, nations, limited liability companies — and only sapiens, therefore, can cooperate in unlimited numbers. A chimpanzee troop caps out where personal acquaintance ends; you can only trust who you know. But strangers who believe the same story can trust each other on sight. Money, gods, empires, laws, corporations, human rights: none of these exists in physics or biology. They are what Harari calls intersubjective realities — real because enough minds agree, and only while they agree. His signature demonstration is Peugeot, the French carmaker: it owns factories, borrows money, and signs contracts, yet you cannot point to it. Destroy every car and every factory, and Peugeot persists; a judge's ritual words could kill it in an afternoon while all its assets stand. The company is a legal fiction that fifty thousand employees and millions of customers treat as real — and that, scaled up, is the operating system of civilisation. Money is simply the most successful story ever told: the only fiction everybody believes, which is why a banknote works between two people who share no god, no king, and no language, and why the currencies of sworn enemies still convert.

That is the Cognitive Revolution, part one of four. The foragers who carried this new software out of Africa were not brutes, and Harari's account of them is deliberately admiring: varied diets, wide skills, arguably more leisure than their descendants — and, as emblem of the new mind, a forty-thousand-year-old figurine of a lion-headed man, a creature that never existed, carved by someone who could imagine it. What happened to everyone else — the Neanderthals and the rest — the book leaves appropriately uncomfortable: some interbreeding, whose traces survive in our DNA, and some or much replacement. The storyteller was not necessarily gentle.

Part two is the book's most famous argument. The Agricultural Revolution, beginning around ten thousand years ago, is conventionally history's triumph: humanity learns to farm, civilisation follows. Harari calls it history's biggest fraud. Grain-by-grain, the evidence he assembles says farming made the average life worse: monotonous calories instead of varied diets, epidemic disease from dense settlement and livestock, back-breaking labour the human body never evolved for, hierarchy and taxation to manage the crowds — and no way back, because population growth burned the bridges. Ten farmers can live where one forager could, so the trap ratchets: each improvement raises the population that now depends on it. Hence his notorious inversion: we did not domesticate wheat; wheat domesticated us. From wheat's point of view — a marginal grass in a corner of the Middle East — the bargain converted a species of clever apes into its global labour force, clearing land, carrying water, and guarding it around the clock. The mechanism deserves its own name, and gets one: the luxury trap. Comforts become necessities and necessities become chains, and the pattern did not stop with grain — it runs straight through washing machines and email, every time-saving device that somehow left nobody with more time. Honesty requires the footnote the book omits: the worse-lives thesis is borrowed — Jared Diamond called agriculture the worst mistake in human history a generation earlier, compressing a real anthropological literature — and it remains a contested position, presented in Sapiens as settled.

With crowds came the need to organise them, and part two ends on the book's sharpest analytical tool. Every large society, Harari argues, rests on an imagined order — and he makes the point by reading two documents side by side: Babylon's ancient law code, which declares the gods have ordained superiors, commoners, and slaves; and the American Declaration of Independence, which declares all men created equal and endowed with rights. Neither hierarchy nor equality is out there in biology, he says; both are fictions that organise millions, enforced by belief where possible and by soldiers where not. History's actual hierarchies — race, caste, gender — are contingencies that hardened into nature, and the section's refrain is blunt: there is no justice in history. You do not have to accept the flattening — philosophers protest, reasonably, that treating all normative orders as equivalent fictions erases the differences that matter most — to feel the tool's power. Ask of any institution: whose story is this, who believes it, who enforces it? That question, once installed, does not uninstall.

Part three explains why the world converged. Thousands of separate human worlds became one, and Harari credits three universal orders, each indifferent to borders. Money, the most tolerant religion, converting between all value systems. Empire, which — whatever its crimes, and the book's ledger of them is real if brisk — standardised languages, laws, coinage, and cultures across its conquests, so thoroughly that most of what any modern culture calls authentic is imperial inheritance. And the universal religions, exporting single truths across ethnic lines. Globalisation, in this reading, is not a departure from history but its arrow: the last stage of a unification that has been running for three thousand years. The chapter's most quietly subversive claim follows: there are no authentic cultures to return to, only earlier mixtures — the chilli in Indian food arrived with Europeans from the Americas, and every purist's golden age turns out to be someone else's empire — so nostalgia for cultural purity is nostalgia for a fiction about fictions.

Part four gives the modern world its origin story. What changed around five hundred years ago, Harari argues, was an admission: premodern knowledge systems claimed completeness — everything important was already in scripture — while modern science institutionalised we do not know, and thereby licensed discovery. But the ideas alone built nothing. The engine was a feedback loop: European empires funded science that improved navigation and conquest, which funded more science; and capitalism supplied the fuel with its own great fiction, credit — trust in a bigger future, which, believed widely enough, manufactures the future it trusts in. Columbus's voyages and the scientific expeditions that followed sailed on the same loop of knowledge, conquest, and capital. Historians of science will tell you the hinge is simplified, and they are right; as a frame for why science, why Europe, why then, it remains the most teachable version anyone has written.

And then the book does the two things that lift it above its genre. First, the audit. Across all this accumulation of power, it asks the question history books never ask: did any of it make anyone happier? The wellbeing science it summarises — at postcard depth, but honestly — says happiness tracks expectations and biochemistry more than objective conditions. Medieval peasants with low expectations and strong communities may have felt no worse than commuters with smartphones; every labour-saving device raised the bar instead of the mood. If contentment has a biological thermostat that success cannot move, then history's whole ledger of conquest and invention may have been, on the only measure that finally matters to the beings living it, a rounding error — which is why the chapter drifts, in its closing pages, toward the one tradition that took craving itself as the problem: the Buddhist analysis Harari practises, where the author's own meditation shows through the prose. And one population's lot unambiguously worsened: the tens of billions of animals in industrial farms, which Harari — a vegan, and unsparing on this — ranks among the greatest crimes in history. Second, the ending. Natural selection, four billion years old, is being replaced by intelligent design — ours. Genetic engineering, brain-machine interfaces, the war on death itself, which the book names the Gilgamesh Project after humanity's oldest epic of refusing mortality. The last sentence became the book's most quoted, and it is a warning: we are self-made gods, more powerful than ever, responsible to no one, and we do not know what we want.

Now the reliability report, because no bestseller of its era earned a more specific one. Galen Strawson, reviewing it as a philosopher, found much to admire and named the flaw permanently: carelessness, exaggeration and sensationalism. Charles Mann, the science journalist, caught the register exactly — a whiff of dorm-room bull sessions, stimulating assertions, often unsourced. The anthropologist C. R. Hallpike delivered the professional's verdict: where Harari's facts are broadly correct they are not new, and where he strikes out on his own he often gets things wrong — not, in Hallpike's judgement, a serious contribution to knowledge. And the evolutionary biologist Darshana Narayanan published an actual error catalogue: animal communication misdescribed as vocal language, in defiance of undergraduate biology; chimpanzees described fighting cheetahs that do not live on the same part of the continent; an Ecuadorian people cited as evidence that states pacify humans, who have in fact been peaceful for fifty years without police or prisons. Her charge — science populism, sensational yarns woven around scientific facts — comes with the most devastating quote in the file, from Harari's own doctoral supervisor: he leapfrogs expert critique by asking questions so large that no one can say, we think this bit's wrong. None of this sank the book; most of it arrived after the tens of millions of copies. But it defines the terms of use precisely. The frames are excellent. The facts are not load-bearing. Read accordingly.

Judged on content alone, as this library judges — the endorsements and the sales excluded — Sapiens rates five point four out of ten: limited. The arithmetic is instructive. Explanatory power and insight both score high: the fictions lens, the luxury trap, and the power-versus-happiness audit are furniture a reader keeps for life. Practical utility is near zero by design. But evidence and calibration drag the total down hard, and documented rather than alleged: an error catalogue, sparse sourcing, and — the deepest flaw — no calibration at all, speculation and consensus delivered in one identical confident register, so the reader cannot tell the load-bearing walls from the scenery. The book teaches you that institutions are stories maintained by confidence, and it is itself a story maintained by confidence. That is either an irony or a final exam, and Harari, to his credit, built the tools to pass it.

So: read the full book if you want the frames at full seductive force — it remains the fastest-reading grand narrative in modern nonfiction, and the set pieces reward the original prose. Stop here if you wanted the four revolutions, the fictions lens, and the calibration the book omits; the ideas transfer completely. Skip it if you need citable facts about human evolution or economics, or if you already know the scholarship it compresses — imagined communities, Diamond's worst mistake, social construction — because you will meet those ideas here, brilliantly staged and uncredited. And whichever you choose, take the book's own habit and finish the job it starts: this week, describe one institution you belong to as a maintained fiction — whose belief sustains it, what enforces it. Then take one confident claim from any big book, this one included, and check it. Harari taught a generation the first move. His critics taught it the second. The complete education is both.
