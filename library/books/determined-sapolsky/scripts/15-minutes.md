Determined begins with a challenge: point to the instant when a choice becomes independent of everything that made the chooser. Robert Sapolsky expects you to fail. Each time a cause appears, another cause stands beneath it. His image is “turtles all the way down”.

Suppose somebody insults a stranger. In the final second, neurons organise speech and movement. In the minutes before, the brain interprets a face, tone, memory, and threat. Hormones, hunger, pain, sleep, and stress change that interpretation. Earlier experiences trained attention and impulse control. Childhood shaped the stress system. Fetal conditions influenced development. Genes interacted with every environment. Culture supplied categories of respect and insult. Evolution built a social primate capable of rage, restraint, imitation, and concern.

These are not separate explanations competing to be the real one. A neurotransmitter was built through gene expression. Gene expression responds to environment. A parent's behaviour carries culture into a developing brain. A current status threat activates machinery shaped across a lifetime. Sapolsky's central scientific claim is cumulative: behaviour is the current edge of one connected causal history.

The argument does not say people never think. Conscious intent can redirect attention, inhibit action, and form a plan. The question is whether intention floats free of causes. Why did this person notice this reason? Why could they hold it in working memory? Why did one value outweigh another? Why was inhibition strong today and weak yesterday? The intention is part of the machinery, not a commander standing outside it.

This is where willpower and grit enter. Effort matters, but the capacity for effort varies. Frontal systems involved in planning and restraint develop under genetic, prenatal, nutritional, emotional, educational, and social influences. Chronic stress can narrow time horizons. Secure practice can build habits. Sleep changes control. A mentor can make persistence imaginable. Success at yesterday's task can alter today's expectation.

Telling someone to practise may help because advice becomes a cause. Practice can strengthen skill and regulation. But when a successful person claims moral ownership of grit, Sapolsky asks them to account for the conditions that made starting and persisting possible. If effort produces achievement, and prior luck helps produce effort, achievement cannot become proof of superior worth.

The first four chapters build this account through brains, intent, and willpower. The next six examine three possible loopholes: chaos, emergence, and quantum indeterminacy. Each receives a primer and then an application to freedom.

Chaos explains why a system governed by rules can become practically unpredictable. Tiny differences in starting conditions amplify until long-range prediction fails. Weather is the familiar case. Its unpredictability does not make a storm free. A chaotic brain may defeat measurement while still evolving through causes. Ignorance about which cause mattered is not evidence that nothing caused the result.

Emergence concerns new properties at higher levels. Wetness belongs to collections of water molecules, not an isolated molecule. Minds, cultures, and decisions can be real patterns that require their own vocabulary even if they depend on lower levels. Sapolsky does not deny higher-level explanation. He denies that a property becomes independent of history merely because it emerges from complexity.

Quantum physics may include indeterminate events. That weakens a simple clockwork picture of the universe, but randomness is not authorship. If a random fluctuation changes which option wins, the person has not gained control. Replacing a determined outcome with a microscopic coin toss does not create responsibility. Sapolsky concludes that none of these phenomena supplies a self capable of choosing otherwise while every relevant condition remains identical.

That conclusion targets contra-causal freedom: a person as a cause not fully produced by prior causes. It does not by itself defeat every account called free will. Compatibilists agree that people are physical and caused. They ask whether an action comes from the person's own values and reasons, responds to evidence, and occurs without coercion or disabling impairment. On this view, freedom is a kind of organised control within causation, not an escape from it.

Sapolsky gives compatibilism less attention than its importance warrants. His test asks for a neuron or brain acting independently of its biological past. No compatibilist expects such a neuron. Showing there is no gap for an uncaused controller can therefore leave the central compatibilist question untouched: why should a caused capacity for reflection, learning, and reasons-responsive action not ground a practical form of responsibility?

The distinction matters because causal histories produce different present capacities. A seizure, an accidental movement, an action under threat, a psychotic episode, a child's impulsive act, and a planned fraud all have causes. They do not involve equal understanding or control. Science can identify these differences. A theory of responsibility must say what they mean rather than assuming that causation makes them morally identical.

Sapolsky calls himself a hard incompatibilist. Whether the universe is deterministic or partly indeterminate, he sees no freedom strong enough for basic desert. The second half of Determined asks how to live if he is right. It begins with the fear that people will run amok.

His answer separates determinism from fatalism. Fatalism says that whatever you do, the same outcome arrives. Determinism says what you do is among the causes of the outcome. A warning can prevent harm. Education can build reasoning. Medication can stabilise a brain. Social trust can widen options. Rules, incentives, and consequences can alter future action. A deterministic world changes constantly because causes interact.

People can also change themselves, provided “themselves” means an organism using inherited and learned capacities. Writing a reminder, avoiding a trigger, asking a friend for help, and practising a response all modify the conditions of a later choice. Nothing becomes less real because it has a mechanism. A thermostat controls temperature without metaphysical freedom; a human exercises vastly richer, reflective control without needing to be self-created.

This preserves practical accountability. Telling the truth about an action, requiring repair, setting a boundary, removing dangerous access, or offering treatment can all change behaviour and protect people. Sapolsky wants to remove one further element: retribution, the belief that an offender's suffering is good because it is deserved.

He compares dangerous behaviour with quarantine. A person who presents a serious risk may need restraint. The reason is protection, not hatred. Restriction should be evidence-based, proportionate, humane, regularly reviewed, and paired with rehabilitation. When danger falls, the justification ends. Deterrence can remain if it works, because a threatened consequence is another cause. The emotional pleasure of making someone pay has no independent justification.

This is a radical but useful separation. Criminal systems often mix incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation, public expression, repair, and vengeance. Naming each purpose allows evidence to replace a single language of punishment. If a long sentence neither protects nor deters nor repairs, “desert” should not rescue it automatically.

The quarantine analogy also creates danger. Predictions of future harm are uncertain and can reproduce bias. Governments can label inconvenient people risky. Indefinite preventive detention may become harsher than a fixed sentence. Human beings have interests, relationships, rights, and the capacity to answer reasons; they are not defective cars. A non-retributive system still needs due process, legal limits, equal protection, independent review, and serious attention to victims.

Sapolsky supports his moral hope with examples of earlier change. Epileptic seizures were once attributed to possession or sin. Greater causal understanding helped replace condemnation with medical response. Mental illness, developmental differences, and addiction have undergone related, incomplete shifts. He asks society to continue: as every behaviour becomes more explicable, hatred and blame should retreat.

The inference is not automatic. Understanding why a seizure occurs shows why ordinary voluntary capacities are absent in that episode. Understanding why an adult formed an intention may instead reveal those capacities operating. Causal explanation can both excuse and confirm agency, depending on what it explains. “We have done this before” demonstrates that responsibility practices can change, not that every distinction should disappear.

The book then confronts the joy of punishment. Humans often find retaliation satisfying. Anger can signal a violation, rally allies, and deter exploitation. But evolved or learned appetite does not establish moral truth. Sapolsky wants us to treat punitive pleasure as another caused impulse to regulate, especially where it produces humiliation, excessive sentences, or neglect of prevention.

The final chapter turns to wealth and poverty. No one selects their genes, prenatal environment, parents, neighbourhood, early health, teachers, historic moment, or initial social position. Nor do they select the early formation of ambition and self-control. The successful person may have worked extremely hard, yet cannot claim to have created the machinery capable of that work from nothing.

This does not tell society exactly how to distribute income or recognition. Incentives can affect behaviour. Expertise and reliability can justify role differences. Gratitude can strengthen relationships without asserting that someone created themselves. Trust can track demonstrated conduct. Sapolsky's strongest distributive conclusion is narrower: luck reaches too deeply into merit for success to justify unlimited entitlement or poverty to justify abandonment.

His larger emotional programme is harder. If no one is ultimately self-authored, hatred of a person makes no more sense to him than hatred of a storm. He wants less shame, pride, resentment, and moralised gratitude. Yet even he admits that he rarely experiences people this way. The admission makes the book humane and exposes a missing account of everyday relationships.

People need to distinguish accident from betrayal, decide whom to trust, request apologies, recognise care, and protect boundaries. These practices need not depend on ultimate desert. They can respond to intention, capacity, relationship, risk, and the effects of communication. Eliminating vindictive hatred does not require pretending that all conduct has the same meaning.

The book's scientific synthesis is its strongest achievement. It makes simplistic stories about genes, character, and choice difficult to sustain. It explains why a hungry, frightened, exhausted, traumatised, or well-supported person does not face the same practical decision. It makes moral luck biological and social without reducing behaviour to one level.

Its method also corrects the familiar split between nature and nurture. Genes do not issue fixed behavioural commands; their activity depends on cells and environments. Environments do not write on blank organisms; bodies differ in sensitivity and response. Culture is learned through nervous systems and reshapes the settings encountered by the next generation. A cause can be biological and social at once. The result is not that genes are destiny, but that no clean column can be removed from the history.

Decision-timing experiments therefore carry less weight than popular accounts often give them. If neural preparation begins before a person reports conscious intention, that challenges a simple conscious initiator. It does not prove that reflection occurring over minutes, years of learning, or reasons encoded in the brain played no causal role. Sapolsky's case is cumulative rather than dependent on one experiment: wherever the window is widened, another formative process appears.

That cumulative strategy is persuasive against a magical gap and less decisive against organised agency. A sailor is caused, trained, embodied, and constrained, yet still steers by sensing error and changing course. The compatibilist says human control is a vastly richer version of that feedback. Sapolsky replies that neither sailor nor capacity is self-created. Both can be true; the argument turns on which truth responsibility requires.

Its main flaw is a mismatch between evidence and conclusion. Science can show how intentions form and how capacities vary. Whether those caused capacities deserve the word freedom is partly conceptual. Whether anyone should suffer, how rights work, and which inequalities are fair are normative questions. Scientific facts constrain those answers but do not generate them alone.

Use Determined in layers. First, accept the causal discipline: before blaming a trait, examine brain, body, development, context, and institution. Second, accept the practical discipline: change causes if you want different outcomes. Third, separate protection and repair from revenge. Fourth, withhold the final metaphysical conclusion until you have compared hard incompatibilism with serious compatibilist and libertarian positions.

Read the original if you want the primers, studies, footnotes, jokes, and the experience of an eminent scientist pushing one causal worldview through every resistance. The fifteen-minute argument is enough to understand the whole claim and why it remains disputed. The most robust takeaway survives either verdict on free will: people differ in capacities they did not simply choose, so judgment should become more curious, consequences more purposeful, success more humble, and social design more attentive to causes.

That shift does not require calling people helpless or denying their present differences. It asks us to treat their reasoning and effort as real causal powers, while refusing to pretend those powers simply appeared from nowhere.
