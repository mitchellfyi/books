Determined asks you to find the moment when a person becomes independent of causes. Robert Sapolsky traces an action backwards instead. A neural event in the last second depends on sensations, hormones, sleep, stress, and earlier learning. Those depend on childhood, fetal conditions, genes, relationships, culture, ecology, and evolution. It is “turtles all the way down”: not one controlling self plus its biology, but one connected causal system.

Conscious intention remains real and can change behaviour. Sapolsky's question is where the intention came from. The ability to notice a reason, inhibit an impulse, or persist through difficulty depends on a brain and history the person did not design. Even grit has a biography. Nutrition, childhood security, present stress, education, models, practice, genes, and frontal development affect who can exert effort and benefit from it. Working still matters, because work changes brains and outcomes. It cannot establish pure moral desert when the capacity to work was unequally caused.

The book then closes three apparent escape routes. Chaos makes a deterministic system unpredictable when tiny differences grow; unpredictability is not freedom. Emergence creates real higher-level properties, but does not insert a self outside the system. Quantum events may be indeterminate, but a random neural event gives a person less control, not more. Sapolsky therefore finds no place for the kind of free will that could have acted independently of the full causal past.

This does not mean nothing can change. Fatalism says an outcome occurs regardless of intervention. Determinism says interventions are causes. Advice, treatment, education, incentives, relationships, and reflection can alter what happens next. The fact that this paragraph may affect a later choice is part of the argument, not an exception to it.

Present capacity still matters. A seizure, accident, act under threat, child's outburst, reckless risk, and planned fraud are all caused, yet differ in awareness and control. Sapolsky's view most clearly attacks ultimate desert: nobody created the kind of brain and history they now possess. It need not erase the practical differences that tell us whether explanation, treatment, a warning, a boundary, or restraint will work.

Sapolsky's moral conclusion is that nobody ultimately deserves praise, blame, privilege, or suffering. Dangerous people may need to be restrained, just as a dangerous medical condition must be contained. Society can protect, deter, treat, rehabilitate, and repair without adding vengeance. He wants criminal justice to resemble humane quarantine: the least harmful effective restriction, review of continuing danger, and release when safe.

He also applies moral luck to inequality. A successful student did not choose the womb, neighbourhood, health, teachers, expectations, or neural capacity that made sustained effort possible. A struggling person did not choose their burdens. Recognising causes should move policy towards prevention and opportunity and move individuals away from hatred, humiliation, and entitlement.

The strongest objection is philosophical. Many compatibilists agree that every action has causes. They call a person free when action flows from their own reasons, values, and capacities without coercion or disabling impairment. On that account, caused reflection is not the opposite of control; it is how control works. Sapolsky largely defines freedom as independence from causation, then shows that no such independence exists. That defeats a supernatural chooser more securely than it defeats every meaningful form of agency.

The ethical leap also needs care. Learning the cause of epilepsy rightly removed blame for seizures, but it does not follow that deliberate harm and a seizure involve the same capacities. Preventive detention brings risks of prediction error and state abuse. A humane system still needs evidence, proportionality, due process, victim protection, and distinctions among accident, coercion, impairment, recklessness, and intent.

The durable lesson is not passivity. Trace more causes before judging character. Separate safety and repair from the wish to inflict suffering. Treat education, incentives, and accountability as tools that shape future behaviour. Hold success with humility because luck reaches into effort. But keep the live question clear: science explains how control is built; philosophy must still decide which kinds of caused control count as freedom and responsibility.

Whichever answer wins, a response should fit the person's present capacity, reduce future harm, address victims and repair relationships, and avoid needless suffering that serves only moral anger.
