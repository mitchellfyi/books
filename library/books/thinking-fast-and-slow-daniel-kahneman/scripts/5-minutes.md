Thinking, Fast and Slow opens with a useful trick: it makes you feel an answer arriving before you decide to produce one. Daniel Kahneman calls automatic mental activity System 1. It reads a face, completes a phrase and detects danger. System 2 is effortful attention: the part recruited for calculation, comparison and restraint. These are characters in an explanation, not two literal brain regions. Their relationship supplies the book's central argument. Fast thought continuously offers impressions; slow thought can check them, but attention is scarce and effort is often avoided. Most judgments therefore begin with intuition and receive only light supervision.

That arrangement is efficient, but it creates predictable errors. Fast thought links whatever is available into a coherent story. Kahneman calls the information problem WYSIATI: what you see is all there is. Evidence that is absent rarely weakens the confidence created by evidence that is present. When the real question is hard, the mind substitutes an easier one. Instead of estimating a company's prospects, it judges whether the chief executive seems impressive. Instead of assessing life satisfaction, it reports the mood of the moment.

Three shortcuts organise the next section. Anchoring pulls an estimate toward a number that happened to be mentioned. Availability makes recent or vivid events feel common. Representativeness judges probability by resemblance to a stereotype, often ignoring base rates and sample size. These shortcuts are not always foolish; they make rapid judgment possible. The danger is using them where statistics matter. Small samples vary more than people expect. Extreme results often move back toward average because luck changes, yet praise or blame receives the credit. A vivid story overwhelms the prior probability.

The same appetite for stories produces overconfidence. After an event, its path looks obvious. A forecast feels strong because its narrative is coherent, not because the evidence predicts well. Project teams take the inside view, imagining their own steps, while ignoring how comparable projects actually ended. Kahneman's strongest corrective is the outside view: choose a relevant reference class, begin with its outcome distribution, then adjust carefully for genuine differences.

Expert intuition also needs conditions. Skill can form where patterns are regular, practice is repeated and valid feedback arrives quickly. A firefighter or chess player may learn real cues. Long-range market or political prediction offers weaker regularity, so confidence proves little. In repeated decisions, a simple formula can beat a nuanced human judge because it applies the same factors consistently.

This is also why merely learning bias names is weak protection. Kahneman studied these errors for decades without believing his own intuition had broadly improved. A checklist, independent estimate or consistent scoring rule changes the conditions of judgment. Private resolve asks limited attention to catch every persuasive impression after it has formed. A process can require the missing comparison before confidence takes over.

Part Four turns to risk. Prospect theory says people evaluate gains and losses from a reference point, not final wealth alone. Sensitivity diminishes as amounts grow, losses often hurt more than equal gains please, and small probabilities receive distorted weight. The same outcome can therefore provoke different choices when framed as survival rather than mortality, or as keeping something rather than giving it up. Loss aversion is important, but later research shows it is not universal or constant across settings.

The final part separates the experiencing self from the remembering self. Experience unfolds moment by moment; memory compresses it, favouring peaks and endings while often neglecting duration. A longer painful procedure can be remembered as better if its ending is milder. That leaves a genuine value conflict: should a decision serve the story remembered or the time actually lived?

Good choices sometimes require measuring both answers instead of choosing silently.

The book earns eight out of ten. Its great contribution is not a catalogue of amusing biases but a decision discipline: ask for base rates, take the outside view, test whether an environment can train intuition, structure repeated judgments and reframe choices. Some social-priming and ego-depletion examples later became replication problems, while the two-system story is too neat as literal science. Read the full book for its experiments, research history and cumulative argument. The shorter account is enough if you mainly need the tools and their limits.
