The Power of Habit begins with a useful correction: repeated behaviour is not simply a test of character. When an action is repeated in a stable setting, features of that setting can begin to trigger the response with little conscious thought. Duhigg turns this into a three-part loop. A cue starts a routine, and the routine produces a reward. Anticipation of that reward creates the craving that pulls the loop forward.

The model is deliberately simple. Habits involve more than three boxes, and addiction, compulsion, skill, and organisational policy are not interchangeable. Yet the loop is valuable because it turns a vague complaint into things you can observe. The time, place, emotional state, people nearby, or immediately preceding action may be the cue. The routine is the exact behaviour. The reward is what the behaviour actually supplies, which may not be obvious.

Duhigg's own afternoon cookie illustrates the method. He assumed he wanted sugar. Instead of relying on that guess, he tested different outcomes: a walk, another snack, coffee, or a conversation. He found that social interruption was the real reward and that the urge arrived at a regular time. His new plan was simple: at half past three, walk to a colleague's desk and talk for ten minutes. Repetition gradually made the substitute easier.

The appendix gives four steps. First, identify the routine in concrete language. Second, experiment with different rewards and check whether the urge remains. Third, isolate the cue by recording location, time, emotion, other people, and the preceding action. Fourth, write an if-then plan that links the cue to a replacement routine serving the real reward. Expect several experiments. Habit formation varies greatly by behaviour and person; there is no reliable twenty-one-day rule.

The main text expands this logic through stories. An amnesic patient who cannot describe his neighbourhood can still follow a learned walking route. Rats running a maze show changing activity in brain systems involved in learned action sequences. Febreze succeeds after marketers attach it to the satisfying end of cleaning. Habit-reversal therapy teaches a nail biter to detect the beginning of the movement and use a competing response. These cases make the framework memorable, though no single example proves the whole model.

Duhigg calls some routines keystone habits because they change surrounding systems. Paul O'Neill's focus on worker safety at Alcoa is the strongest example. Rapid injury reporting forced information to travel from the factory floor to senior leaders. Fixing safety therefore exposed broken equipment, weak training, and poor communication. The lesson is not that one magical habit transforms everything. Choose a routine that improves feedback and makes other defects visible.

The Starbucks chapter makes a related point: decide how to respond before pressure arrives. Staff rehearse scripts for predictable conflict so the behaviour is available when attention is strained. This if-then planning remains useful. The chapter's explanation of willpower as a muscle-like resource is less secure. Large later preregistered studies found little or no reliable ego-depletion effect in their tested tasks.

The book then widens habit until it sometimes becomes a loose word for any repeated social pattern. Hospitals preserve authority through routine. Crises can expose dangerous rules. Target uses purchase data to predict disrupted shopping patterns and time marketing around them. The prediction project is documented, but the famous anonymous story about a father discovering his daughter's pregnancy is less verifiable than its retelling suggests. The Montgomery boycott section adds friendship, social expectations, and group identity, but cannot replace political history with a habit loop.

Charles Duhigg is an investigative journalist rather than a neuroscientist or clinician. His strength is joining research to vivid reporting. His weakness is that selected success stories can make one explanation look decisive. The book is engaging but long: much of its practical value sits in the short appendix.

Read it if stories help you remember a model or if you want its organisational and ethical cases. A summary is enough if your goal is to change one bounded routine. Start with observation, test the reward, alter the cue where possible, plan a competing response, and repeat it in a stable setting. For addiction, gambling harm, eating disorders, or compulsions, do not mistake this starting framework for specialist treatment. Content rating: 6.8 out of 10.
