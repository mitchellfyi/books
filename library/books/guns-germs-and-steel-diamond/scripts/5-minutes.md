In 1972, the New Guinean politician Yali asked Jared Diamond why white people had brought so much material wealth to New Guinea while local people had much less of it. Diamond spent the book looking for an answer that did not depend on supposed differences in intelligence or worth. His question is not simply why Europeans won particular battles. It is why societies on one continent acquired the tools of conquest earlier.

The book separates immediate causes from deep causes. At Cajamarca in 1532, a small Spanish force captured the Inca emperor Atahuallpa. The immediate advantages included steel weapons, horses, writing and political organisation. Smallpox had also reached the empire before the Spaniards, killing its previous ruler and helping to cause a civil war. Diamond then asks where those advantages came from.

His first answer is food production. Farming creates surpluses, which can support soldiers, rulers, craftspeople and scribes. Yet farming can only begin with useful wild plants and animals. Diamond argues that Eurasia had an unusually strong starting set: many large-seeded grasses and thirteen of the fourteen major domestic mammals in his classification. He calls domestication an Anna Karenina problem. A useful animal must pass every test, including diet, growth, breeding, temperament and social behaviour. One failure is enough. A zebra resembles a horse but is difficult and dangerous to manage; its failure says nothing about the skill of the people living beside it.

His second answer is continental shape. Eurasia stretches mainly east to west, so crops and animals could often spread through similar seasons and day lengths. Africa and the Americas stretch mainly north to south, with major deserts, tropics and climate changes in the way. Diamond says this slowed the spread of food production and technology. The claim is memorable, although a later quantitative study challenged its strong form.

His third answer is disease. Dense farming populations lived close to livestock and repeatedly suffered epidemics. Over time, surviving populations gained some resistance. Societies in the Americas had fewer domestic animals and therefore fewer crowd diseases. When the continents met, Old World infections caused enormous losses in populations without prior exposure. Germs often travelled faster than the conquerors.

The middle of the book builds a chain from food to power. Surplus food supports specialists. Larger populations create more inventions and more chances to copy them. Writing helps states keep records. Central governments collect resources and organise armies. Guns, germs and steel are therefore the visible results of earlier ecological advantages, not Diamond's deepest causes.

The final sections apply this framework to Australia, New Guinea, East Asia, the Pacific, the Americas and Africa. They give the argument breadth, but also repeat it. The book reads more like a long scientific case than a narrative history. It is strongest when comparing continents over thousands of years and weaker when explaining why one Eurasian state later outperformed another.

Diamond tries to test broad claims with natural experiments. Polynesian settlers carried related cultures to islands with very different sizes, climates and resources, then developed different forms of farming, population and government. The comparison is useful because it holds ancestry more nearly constant while changing the environment. It also shows the method's limit: historical cases can suggest causes, but they cannot isolate variables as cleanly as a laboratory experiment.

That weakness drives most criticism. Historians argue that culture can change environmental limits. Anthropologists say Diamond gives too little weight to Indigenous alliances, colonial decisions and responsibility. Economists show that institutions helped reverse the fortunes of many former colonies, limiting any account based on fixed geography. Other research still finds links between early biogeography and later development. The evidence therefore narrows the framework rather than simply proving or disproving it.

The content earns six point four out of ten. Its explanatory structure and insight are strong. Its evidence is mixed, its modern extension is under-calibrated, and more than four hundred pages repeat a fairly compact idea. Read the full book for the regional comparisons, maps and cumulative case. A shorter account is enough if you mainly want the causal chain. It is not detailed colonial history, a complete theory of modern inequality, or a book centred on individual lives. Its lasting lesson is precise: material power does not prove biological superiority, but environmental starting conditions are only the beginning of history, not the whole of it.
