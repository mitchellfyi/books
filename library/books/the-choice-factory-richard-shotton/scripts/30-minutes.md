Your day is a factory of decisions. What to grab for breakfast; which queue looks faster; which app deserves the download; what that price actually means; whether the ad on the platform is telling the truth; which brand of the forty on the shelf ends up in your basket without your ever quite choosing it. The uncomfortable premise of this book is that most of those decisions are not entirely yours — they are steered by a repertoire of psychological shortcuts as old as the species and as current as this morning's commute — and its practical promise is that the repertoire can be learned, tested, and used. The Choice Factory: 25 Behavioural Biases That Influence What We Buy was published by Harriman House in February 2018, a 220-page paperback by Richard Shotton: a Londoner who spent two decades planning and buying media inside advertising agencies before founding Astroten, a consultancy applying behavioural science to marketing for clients like Google, Facebook, and Sky. The book became the advertising industry's behavioural primer almost on contact. It went to number one on Amazon, sold past a hundred thousand copies in fifteen languages, was voted first in the famous BBH agency poll of the best advertising books ever written — ahead of the canon it summarises — won the Business Book Awards' sales-and-marketing category, and earned Shotton an honorary lifetime fellowship of the IPA, the British advertising industry's professional body. It is, by adoption if not by intention, the book handed to every new marketer in London, and increasingly everywhere else.

Its structure is its pitch, and the pitch is disarming: no grand theory, no manifesto — just one ordinary day, followed from the morning platform to the last impression before sleep, with a bias caught in the act at each stop. The day belongs to a lightly sketched everywoman whose decisions the chapters shadow — which train, which snack, which insurance, which round in the pub — so that by the evening the reader has watched a fully ordinary life get steered forty times before dinner without a single dramatic moment. The mundanity is the message: this is not a book about con artists and casinos; it is about Tuesdays. Twenty-five short chapters, each running the same tight loop. First, the classic experiment, told quickly. Second — and this is the book's signature — Shotton's own quick field test: a cheap modern replication run on real consumers, checking whether the old finding still moves a contemporary shopping public. Third, the application: what a working marketer could do about it this week. The loop repeats twenty-five times, the chapters average eight pages, and the whole thing reads in an afternoon — a deck of cards rather than an argument, built deliberately for dipping, and the closest thing to a summary format that a full book can have.

Two things about the author position the whole project. First, he is a media man, not a creative: two decades spent deciding where and when advertising ran, which is why the book's centre of gravity is context — timing, placement, mood, price architecture — rather than the copywriter's beloved words. Second, he is a collector: the book distils years of a running habit in which Shotton read the original studies — not the summaries of summaries — and tried them out on real audiences, accumulating the test files that became the chapters. The result reads less like an author announcing a theory than a practitioner opening his notebook, and the industry trusted it for exactly that reason.

Begin where the book begins, on the commute, because the first bias is the book's founding stance. A stranger shoves past you onto the train and your mind files them instantly: rude person. Not person late for a hospital appointment; not person having the worst week of their year — rude person, character over circumstance, verdict before evidence. That reflex is the fundamental attribution error, and Shotton opens with it because it is the marketing industry's own occupational disease. Brands attribute buying behaviour to what consumers are like — the loyal customer, the promiscuous switcher, the engaged fan — when the real driver, again and again, is the situation the person was in: the hour, the mood, the context, the shelf. The first lesson of the book is therefore not a tactic but a posture: study contexts, not characters. Target moments and moods, not imagined personality types. Shotton's own tests found the identical advertisement performing differently by time of day, surrounding media, and reported mood — the person hadn't changed between exposures; the context had, and the context was the variable that mattered. Media context gets its own chapter-level result: the same ad lands differently beside comedy than beside hard news, absorbing the emotional temperature of its surroundings — which makes where and when as much a part of the message as the words, and makes cheap media that poisons the message expensive.

Now the day accelerates, and the buying biases arrive in convoy. At the app store: social proof, the herd as evidence. A million downloads is the argument; popularity claims — truthfully made — move products that quality claims cannot, because in uncertainty we outsource judgment to everyone else's choices. The book's practical refinement is the trap institutions spring on themselves: negative social proof. Announce that everyone is doing the bad thing — so many people miss their appointments, litter is everywhere, tax dodging is rampant — and you have just advertised the bad behaviour's popularity, normalising exactly what you meant to shame. The fix is always the same inversion: make the desirable behaviour the visible majority — most people pay on time — and let the herd instinct pull in the direction you want. At the till: charm pricing. Four ninety-five is not five pounds. We read prices left to right, anchor on the first digit, and file the price in a mental bucket a category cheaper — a penny's difference doing a pound's work. Beside it, price relativity, anchoring's retail face: the same bottle of wine reads as a bargain or an extravagance depending entirely on its neighbours, which is why menus carry one absurdly priced dish nobody is expected to order — it exists to make the second-most-expensive dish reasonable — and why the mid-priced option in any lineup is usually the architecture's intended product. The compromise effect formalises the trick: offered two options, people split; add a premium third and the crowd migrates to the middle, which has silently moved up — so the surest way to sell more of something is often to introduce something dearer above it. Nothing about the product changed. The ladder did. And beneath every price, expectation effects that shade into outright placebo: in the studies the book deploys, the same wine tastes measurably better when drinkers believe it cost more — brain-imaging versions show reward regions responding to the believed price, not the liquid — and energy drinks bought at a discount produce measurably worse performance than the identical drink at full price. The label writes the experience before the product gets a vote, which is why price is never just what you charge: it is part of what the customer consumes.

The halo effect earns its stop on the tour because it quietly finances an entire industry: one salient positive attribute — beauty in the presenter, a single brilliant feature in the product, sheer familiarity in the brand — spills over into unrelated judgments, so the attractive spokesperson makes the claims feel truer and the well-designed box makes the gadget feel faster. Advertising's oldest instincts, the book shows, are this effect operationalised, and its modern refinement is knowing what carries halos in your category: sometimes beauty, often confidence, increasingly design. The confirmation trap follows close behind, aimed at the marketer rather than the market: once a campaign is loved internally, every ambiguous signal gets read as vindication — which is half the reason the book keeps demanding tests, since the test is the only reader of results with no favourite.

The book's most famous chapter arrives with the evening's advertising, and it deserves its fame: the pratfall effect. In the classic experiment, a highly capable quiz contestant who clumsily spills coffee over himself becomes more likeable than the identical flawless contestant — competence plus a small stumble beats competence alone, because perfection reads as inhuman, unrelatable, and faintly suspect. Shotton traces advertising's most counterintuitive golden thread from that finding: the celebrated campaigns built on admitted flaws. The little car introduced to giant-car America under a headline calling it a lemon. The lager sold, at the height of price competition, as reassuringly expensive. The rental company that led with being number two — and therefore trying harder. Each worked by credibility transfer: a brand that concedes something against its own interest must be telling the truth about the rest, so the admitted weakness purchases belief for every other claim in the campaign — and differentiation comes free, since no competitor will copy an admission. The boundary condition is stated plainly: the flaw must be peripheral and the competence signal strong. A surgeon can charmingly spill coffee; a surgeon cannot charmingly drop the scalpel. And the chapter doubles as the book's own ethic in miniature: the honest version, deployed with intelligence, sells better anyway — persuasion and truthfulness are not opposites; laziness and truthfulness are.

Language gets its own strange chapter via the rhyme-as-reason effect, the finding Shotton delights in most: the same claim is rated more truthful when it rhymes — caution and measure will bring you treasure outperforms its plain-prose twin on believability, though nobody will admit the rhyme did it. Fluency is the mechanism: statements that are easy to process feel true, which is why slogans, alliteration, and simple words outsell precision, why the best-remembered advertising lines scan like nursery rhymes, and why the curse-of-knowledge jargon of the average brief is not just ugly but actively disbelieved. Order matters the same way: primacy effects mean the first item in a list colours everything after it — the first attribute mentioned becomes the lens — so the sequence of an argument is part of the argument, on packs, in pitches, and in the search results where position is destiny.

The homeward stretch collects the remaining heavy hitters. Scarcity: the offer that expires, the stock that dwindles, the reflex that reads disappearing as valuable — refined by testing into the rule that scarcity claims work when plausible and specific; "limited edition" is wallpaper, "three left at this price" is a lever. Habit: a huge share of buying is not deciding at all but repeating — same time, same shelf, same coffee, context cueing action with the mind elsewhere — which means the marketer's richest moments are the breaks in routine: the house move, the new job, the new baby, the January, when frozen habits thaw and can be re-formed around a different brand; Shotton's advice is to spend disproportionately on people in transition, because everyone else is barely listening. The evidence he marshals is life-event data: switching rates for utilities, supermarkets, and papers spike around moves and milestones and flatline between them, so a pound aimed at a mover buys multiples of a pound aimed at the settled — targeting by moment beating targeting by demographic, the book's context thesis paying cash. Distinctiveness, the von Restorff effect: the item that violates the pattern is the item recalled, which is the scientific case for zigging when the category zags, and the quiet indictment of every category's samey, safe, expensively invisible advertising. Wit: the industry had drifted into treating funny as unserious, and the book assembles the opposite case — humour is memorable, disarming, and a costly signal of confidence, and its decline in advertising was fashion, not findings. The curse of knowledge: once you know a thing you cannot simulate not knowing it, which is why experts write briefs full of category jargon that communicates nothing to the humans buying the product, and why the book prescribes testing every line on people who know nothing — the only readers who resemble the audience. And the peak-end rule closes the day: experiences are remembered not as their running average but by their most intense moment and their final one, so endings are disproportionately the memory — the checkout, the last mile, the customer-service goodbye — and a brand's exit music matters more than its overture. The rule's practical corollaries cascade: never let the last touch be the invoice; fix the worst moment before polishing the average one, because the peak — good or bad — is doing outsized work; and if an experience must contain pain, front-load it and end warm, since the memory, not the experience, is what returns to buy again. The customer who queued twenty minutes but was walked out with a small kindness remembers a good visit; the arithmetic of moments is not fair, and the book's advice is to stop pretending it is and design for the memory.

Personalisation gets the day's most flattering chapter: the cocktail-party effect, by which your own name cuts through a noisy room your attention had entirely filtered out — the reason names in subject lines lift open rates, the reason relevance beats reach, and the reason the book counsels talking to someone rather than broadcasting at everyone. Its dark twin is the creepiness boundary Shotton's tests keep finding: personalisation that helps is welcomed; personalisation that reveals surveillance repels — the same mechanism, tipped past the point where the customer wonders how you knew. And framing rounds out the toolkit's arithmetic: identical facts land differently by their clothes — the ninety-five per cent fat-free versus the five per cent fat, the loss avoided versus the gain foregone — because minds respond to descriptions, not statistics, and every number in commerce arrives dressed by someone.

Collect the roster and a pattern emerges that is the book's real redirection: the heavy-lifting biases are environmental, not verbal. When the offer arrives, what sits beside it, what the default is, who else appears to be choosing it, what mood the medium has induced — these move outcomes more than any copy tweak, which means marketing effort is systematically misallocated toward wordsmithing while the leverage sits in architecture. Readers of the policy literature will recognise the finding: it is the same lesson choice-design taught government, arriving via the supermarket. And the book's framing of the biases themselves is kinder than the word suggests, which matters for using it well. These are not defects; they are shortcuts — the compressions any finite mind must run to survive a thousand daily choices — and they are usually right, which is exactly why they are exploitable. Nobody deliberates over toothpaste. The brand available to a two-second, habit-driven, context-cued decision wins, and the elaborate rational persuasion imagined in the strategy deck was never read by anyone. Respecting that is not cynicism about consumers; it is accuracy about attention.

The book's real distinction, though — the thing that separates it from every airport summary of the same studies — is the middle step of its chapter loop. Shotton does not simply cite a fifty-year-old laboratory result and move on, the habit that embarrassed a decade of pop behavioural writing. He reruns the classics as cheap modern field tests on real consumers: quick panel experiments checking whether charm pricing still moves modern shoppers, whether social-proof phrasings still shift real claims, whether the pratfall still charms outside the laboratory. Sometimes the classic passes cleanly. Sometimes it holds only under conditions, and he reports the conditions. The book was published in 2018, precisely as the replication crisis was working through behavioural science's greatest hits — priming effects collapsing, famous results shrinking under preregistration — and Shotton's test-first stance turned out to be the genre's best available answer: not blind faith in the canon, not blanket dismissal, but cheap, continuous, local verification. And it carries the book's most valuable instruction, aimed explicitly at its own reader: do not trust the lore — including this book's. Test it on your own audience, at your own price points, in your own category, before you bet the budget. The twenty-five biases are the deck; the testing habit is the game.

The test-first stance also deserves its practical unpacking, because the book's advice is cheaper than most readers assume and the chapter loop models it. A directional field test needs: two versions differing in one thing — the price ending, the social-proof line, the admitted flaw; an audience sample from an online panel, a split email list, or a divided landing page; and a count. Hundreds of respondents, a day's turnaround, coffee-budget money. That is not journal science and does not pretend to be — no preregistration, convenience samples, noise everywhere — but it answers the only question the meeting actually has: does this move our audience, in our category, at our price point? Shotton's deeper point is institutional: advertising began as a testing discipline — the mail-order pioneers counted coupons and knew to the penny which headline paid — and drifted, over decades of brand theatre and awards culture, into confident opinion. The book's whole programme is a homecoming: not new science imported into marketing, but marketing remembering it used to count.

Then there is the combative streak that turned a friendly primer into a trade-press event. Shotton's consistent move, chapter by chapter and column by column, is to ask fashionable industry orthodoxies for their evidence — and the fashion that fared worst was purpose-led branding, the doctrine that consumers reward brands for elevated social missions. The evidence for it, tested rather than asserted, kept coming up thin, and the trade headline recorded the impact: Shotton's Choice Factory brutally dismantles brand purpose. The effectiveness wing of the advertising industry — the faction that had spent a decade insisting campaigns be judged by results rather than awards — adopted the book as its manual, and the endorsement roster reads like that movement's letterhead. Mark Ritson, the marketing professor and columnist, delivered the documented verdict: a short but powerful tome from which you can learn how marketing actually influences consumers. Seth Godin called it actionable, memorable, and powerful. Dave Trott, advertising's plainest-spoken columnist, praised it for exactly the thing the trade needed: research and sociology impacting real life, in simple words and examples real people can understand — which, from the industry's great enemy of pretension, was the certification that mattered. And Rory Sutherland — advertising's flamboyant behavioural evangelist, whose own book Alchemy shares a shelf with this one in every agency — called it a Haynes manual for understanding consumer behaviour. The Sutherland comparison locates the book precisely. Alchemy and The Choice Factory are siblings and opposites: Sutherland supplies the magic — the counterintuitive leaps, the butterfly effects, the case for irrational solutions — while Shotton supplies the evidence — the tests, the boundaries, the sober replication. One argues that logic is overrated; the other checks. Read together, they are the advertising bookshelf's id and superego, and the pairing remains the best two-book education the industry offers.

There is also a chapter's worth of defence in the book for the person on the receiving end, and it deserves collecting, because every lever reverses into a shield. Knowing charm pricing, you round up before judging; knowing anchors, you cover the neighbouring prices and ask what the thing is worth to you; knowing the compromise effect, you notice that the middle option was built to be chosen and audit it hardest; knowing scarcity's rules, you translate "selling fast" into "selling"; knowing the pratfall, you enjoy the charming admission and still check the substance; knowing the peak-end rule, you recognise that the warm goodbye is engineered precisely because it is what you will remember instead of the wait. None of this makes a person bias-proof — the book is clear that knowing about a shortcut rarely disables it, since the shortcuts are attention itself — but it converts a consumer from the factory's raw material into something closer to a quality inspector: still on the line, but taking notes.

Now the honest ledger, because a book this useful deserves a precise one. First, the roster is the standard set: readers arriving from Cialdini's Influence or the Kahneman literature will recognise nearly every bias by its first name; the freshness is the field tests, the advertising case files, and the compression — not new science, and the book never claims otherwise. Second, the chapters are deliberately thin: eight pages per bias buys a scene, a study, a test, and a page of application, with boundary conditions and failure modes mostly left as exercises — fine for a deck of starting hypotheses, dangerous only for a reader who mistakes it for settled doctrine. Third, the quick panel tests are directional screens, not journal science: no effect sizes, no preregistration, samples of convenience — honest about what they are, which is a practitioner's filter, and useful precisely at that grade. Fourth, the classic corpus varies in replication health — some members, like defaults and social proof, robust; others, especially the priming-adjacent, graded down since — and while test-first is the correct answer in principle, its application across chapters is uneven. Fifth, the conflict the text never audits: a consultant popularising the toolkit he sells has an interest in the toolkit's reputation — the same structural caveat this shelf attaches to every practitioner-author, priced rather than pretended away. Judged on content, The Choice Factory rates six point two out of ten: worthwhile, and the arithmetic explains the grade. Practical utility and efficiency score highest — seven-and-a-half apiece — because twenty-five usable ideas in two hundred and twenty fast pages is the best applicable-ideas-per-minute ratio on the entire persuasion row, and every chapter ends in something runnable this week. Evidence lands mid-table: the field-test method is the genre's best practice, marked down for panel-grade standards and a classic corpus of mixed replication health. Insight is the honest dock — the roster is the known canon, operationalised rather than extended — and calibration sits with it: boundaries exist per chapter but the systematic reckoning is left to the reader. The composite verdict: not the deepest book on its shelf, but pound for pound the most usable, lifted above its airport-book neighbours by the one habit they all lack — checking.

So, the decision. Read the full book if you market, advertise, price, or sell anything at all — and that includes founders writing their own copy, freelancers setting their own rates, and anyone with a pricing page. It is an afternoon's first read and a permanent desk reference afterward; the paperback earns its desk space, and the dip-in deck format defeats the audiobook, so take it in print. Its sequel, The Illusion of Choice, adds another round of levers in the same format if the first deck runs dry — and the two Sutherland-Shotton shelves pair naturally with this collection's Influence and Nudge: Cialdini for the compliance levers, Thaler and Sunstein for the defaults, Shotton for the tested retail application, one persuasion education across four registers. Stop here if you wanted the method and the standouts — test before trusting; context beats copy; flaws admitted sell; beware negative social proof; respect the peak and the end; spend on people in transition — because the deck's best cards are now in your hand. Skip it only if you own the Cialdini-Kahneman canon and want new science rather than operationalised craft — or if commercial persuasion is genuinely not your problem, in which case borrow it once anyway, in self-defence, because every technique in it is currently being run on you by someone who read it.

And take the book's own move home, because it is the one part guaranteed to replicate. If you sell: pick one bias from the deck — charm pricing, social proof, the pratfall admission — and design the five-minute test that would check it on your actual audience. Two versions of the price, the claim, the flaw; a cheap panel or a split email; count what happens. Whichever way it comes out, notice what you just did: you replaced an opinion with a result, at almost no cost, in a meeting where everyone else brought only opinions — and once you have felt that advantage land, you will never fully go back to arguing unarmed. If you sell nothing: run the consumer's version. Catch one bias operating on you today — the anchor parked beside the price, the "only three left," the brand charmingly confessing a flaw, the queue that made you want in — and name it before you feel it. The factory runs all day, every day, on all of us, whether or not anyone is minding the machines — it was running before this book, and it will run after. Shotton's book is the tour of the floor, the manual for the levers, and — read properly, from either side of the counter — the safety briefing. The only people it doesn't fool twice, on the shop floor or in the aisles, are the ones who test.
