Your day is a factory of decisions — what to grab for breakfast, which queue to join, which app to trust, what that price means, whether to believe the ad on the platform — and the uncomfortable premise of this book is that most of those decisions are not entirely yours. The Choice Factory: 25 Behavioural Biases That Influence What We Buy, published by Harriman House in 2018, is the work of Richard Shotton, a Londoner who spent two decades buying and planning media in advertising agencies before founding Astroten, a consultancy that applies behavioural science to marketing for clients like Google, Facebook, and Sky. The book became the advertising industry's behavioural primer almost on contact: a hundred thousand copies in fifteen languages, first place in a famous agency poll of the best advertising books ever written, a Business Book Awards category win, and the standard first recommendation handed to anyone starting out in marketing. Its structure is its pitch. Follow one ordinary day, from the shove on the morning platform to the last impression before sleep, and catch twenty-five psychological biases in the act — one per short chapter, each run through the same tight loop: the classic experiment, then Shotton's own quick field test on real consumers, then the application a working marketer could try this week.

Begin where the book begins, on the commute. A stranger shoves past you onto the train, and your mind files them instantly: rude person. Not person late for a hospital visit, not person having the worst morning of their year — rude person, character over circumstance. That reflex is the fundamental attribution error, and Shotton opens with it because it is the marketer's own occupational disease: brands attribute buying behaviour to what consumers are like — loyal, promiscuous, "engaged" — when the real driver is usually the situation they were in. The first lesson of the book is therefore not a tactic but a stance: study contexts, not characters. Target moods and moments, not imagined personality types. His own tests found the same ad performing differently by time, mood, and setting — the person hadn't changed; the context had.

Now the day accelerates, and the biases arrive in convoy. At the app store, social proof: we outsource judgment to the herd, so a million downloads is the argument, and popularity claims — truthfully made — move products that quality claims cannot. The book's practical refinement is negative social proof, the trap institutions fall into constantly: announce that everyone is doing the bad thing — "so many people miss appointments," "litter is everywhere" — and you have just advertised the bad behaviour's popularity, normalising exactly what you meant to shame. At the till, charm pricing: four ninety-five is not five pounds; a penny relocates the price into a cheaper mental bucket, because we read prices left to right and file them by first digit. Beside it, price relativity — anchoring: the same bottle reads as a bargain or an extravagance depending entirely on its neighbours, which is why menus carry one absurdly priced dish nobody orders and why the mid-priced option is the architecture's real product. And behind every price, expectation effects that shade into placebo: the same wine tastes measurably better when drinkers believe it cost more; the label writes the flavour before the tongue gets a vote.

The book's most famous chapter arrives with the evening's advertising: the pratfall effect. In the classic experiment, a highly capable quiz contestant who clumsily spills coffee becomes more likeable than the identical contestant who doesn't — competence plus a small flaw beats competence alone, because perfection reads as inhuman and slightly suspect. Shotton traces advertising's counterintuitive golden thread from that finding: the celebrated campaigns built on admitted flaws. The car sold under the headline calling it a lemon; the beer sold as reassuringly expensive; the rental firm that led with being number two — and therefore trying harder. The mechanism is credibility transfer: a brand that concedes something against its own interest must be telling the truth about the rest, so the admitted weakness buys belief for every other claim. The boundary condition matters, and the book states it: the flaw must be peripheral, and the competence signal must be strong — a surgeon can charmingly spill coffee; a surgeon cannot charmingly drop the scalpel. The chapter doubles as the book's own ethic in miniature: the honest version, deployed intelligently, sells better anyway.

Scarcity gets its chapter on the way home — the offer that expires, the stock that dwindles, the reflex that reads disappearing as valuable — with the field-test refinement that scarcity claims work best when they are plausible and specific: "limited" is wallpaper; "three left at this price" is a lever. Habit gets the day's quietest chapter: a huge share of buying is not deciding at all but repeating, cued by context — same time, same shelf, same coffee — which means the marketer's richest moments are the breaks in routine, the house move, the new job, the January, when habits unfreeze and can be re-formed around a different brand. And humour earns its defence: the industry had drifted into treating funny as unserious, while the evidence runs the other way — wit is memorable, disarming, and a costly signal of confidence, and its decline in advertising was fashion, not findings.

Threaded through the day are the memory and attention biases that decide what survives the evening. The peak-end rule: experiences are remembered not as their average but by their most intense moment and their final one — so the last impression is disproportionately the impression, and a brand's exit music matters more than its overture. Distinctiveness: the item that violates the pattern is the item recalled, which is the scientific case for zigging when the category zags — and the reason so much safe, samey advertising is expensively invisible. The curse of knowledge: once you know a thing, you cannot simulate not knowing it, which is why experts write briefs full of jargon that communicates nothing, and why the book prescribes testing copy on people who know nothing — the only readers who resemble the audience. And mood as targeting: Shotton's tests found receptivity swinging with the hour, the day, and the emotional context of the surrounding media — the same message landing differently beside comedy than beside news — making when and where as much a part of the message as the words.

A word on what the biases are, collectively, because the book's framing is kinder than the word suggests. These are not defects; they are shortcuts — the compressions any finite mind must run to get through a thousand daily choices without seizing up — and they are usually right, which is exactly why they are exploitable. The marketer's edge, in Shotton's telling, is not manipulating broken minds but respecting how normal minds actually allocate attention: nobody deliberates over toothpaste, so the brand available to a two-second, habit-driven, context-cued decision wins, and the elaborate rational case the strategy deck imagines the consumer weighing was never read by anyone.

The book's real distinction, though, is not the roster — it is the middle step of every chapter's loop. Shotton does not merely cite a fifty-year-old laboratory result and move on, the habit that embarrassed a decade of pop psychology. He reruns the classics as cheap modern field tests: quick panel experiments on real consumers, checking whether charm pricing still moves modern shoppers, whether social-proof wording still shifts real claims, whether the old effect holds, weakens, or needs conditions. Sometimes the classics pass; sometimes they need qualifying, and he says so. Published in 2018, exactly as the replication crisis was humbling behavioural science's greatest hits, that stance aged better than nearly anything else in the genre — and it carries the book's most valuable, most transferable instruction, aimed at its own reader: do not trust the lore, including this book's. Test it on your own audience, cheaply, before you bet the budget. The bias chapters are the deck; the testing habit is the game.

And the book has a combative streak that turned it into a trade-press event. Shotton's consistent move is to ask fashionable industry orthodoxies for their evidence, and the fashion that fared worst was purpose-led branding — the doctrine that consumers buy from brands with elevated social missions. The trade headline said it plainly: Shotton's Choice Factory brutally dismantles brand purpose. The book's evidence-first wing of the advertising industry adopted it as their manual, and the endorsement roster reads like the effectiveness movement's letterhead. Mark Ritson, the marketing professor and columnist, called it — in his documented verdict — a short but powerful tome from which you can learn how marketing actually influences consumers. Rory Sutherland, advertising's flamboyant behavioural evangelist, called it a Haynes manual for understanding consumer behaviour — and the Sutherland comparison locates the book precisely, because his own Alchemy is this book's sibling and opposite: Sutherland supplies the magic, the butterfly effects and the glorious leaps; Shotton supplies the evidence, the tests and the sober boundaries. Read together they are the advertising bookshelf's id and superego, and the pairing is the best two-book education the industry offers.

Now the honest ledger, because a book this useful deserves a precise one. First: the roster is the standard set. Readers arriving from Cialdini's Influence or Kahneman's heuristics will recognise nearly every bias; the freshness is the field tests, the ad-industry case files, and the compression — not new science. Second: the chapters are deliberately thin. Eight pages per bias means a scene, a study, a test, and a page of application; boundary conditions and failure modes are mostly left as an exercise, which is fine for a deck of starting hypotheses and dangerous for anyone who mistakes it for settled doctrine. Third: the quick panel tests are directional screens, not journal science — no effect sizes, no preregistration, honest about being a practitioner's filter. Fourth: the classic corpus the book deals in varies in replication health — some members robust, some graded down since — and while the test-first method is the correct answer in principle, it is applied unevenly across chapters. And fifth: a consultant popularising the toolkit he sells has an interest the text never audits — the same conflict this shelf has noted in other practitioner books, worth pricing rather than pretending away. Judged on content, The Choice Factory rates six point three out of ten: worthwhile — the highest count of immediately applicable ideas per minute on the entire persuasion shelf, held below the top tier by familiar material and deliberate thinness.

So, the decision. Read the full book if you market, advertise, price, or sell anything at all: it is an afternoon's first read, a permanent desk reference afterward, and the fastest existing route from the bias literature to something you can test this week — the paperback earns its desk space, and the dip-in format defeats the audiobook. Stop here if you wanted the method and the standouts: test before trusting; context beats copy; flaws admitted sell; watch the negative social proof; respect the peak and the end. Skip it if you own the Cialdini-Kahneman canon and want new science rather than operationalised craft — or if commercial persuasion simply is not your problem, in which case read it anyway once, in self-defence, because every technique in it is currently being run on you.

And take the book's own move home, because it is the part guaranteed to replicate. Pick one bias from the deck — charm pricing, social proof, the pratfall — and design the five-minute test that would check it on your actual audience: two versions of the price, the claim, the admission; a cheap panel or a split email; count what happens. If you sell nothing, run the consumer's version instead: catch one bias operating on you today — the anchor beside the price, the "only two left," the flaw a brand charmingly admits — and name it before you feel it. Then, whichever way it comes out, notice what you just did: you replaced an opinion with a result, at almost no cost, in an argument where everyone else brought opinions. That habit — not any bias, not any tactic — is what Shotton is really selling, and it is why the book outlived the fashion cycle that usually claims marketing titles. The factory runs all day, every day, on all of us, whether or not anyone is minding the machines. The only people it doesn't fool twice are the ones who test.
