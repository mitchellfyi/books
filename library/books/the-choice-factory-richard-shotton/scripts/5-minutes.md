Your day is a factory of decisions, and most of them are not entirely yours. That is the premise of The Choice Factory, Richard Shotton's 2018 book — two decades in London media agencies, then a consultancy applying behavioural science for clients like Google and Sky. The structure is the pitch: follow one ordinary day, and catch twenty-five psychological biases in the act. The commuter who shoves past you at the station gets filed as a rude person rather than a person having a bad morning — the fundamental attribution error, and the first marketing lesson: judge contexts, not consumers. The app you download because a million others did — social proof. The coffee that costs four ninety-five and reads as "four-something" — charm pricing, a penny relocating the price into a cheaper mental bucket. Each of the twenty-five chapters runs the same tight loop: the classic experiment, then Shotton's own quick field test on real consumers, then the application a marketer could run this week.

That middle step is the book's real distinction, and its most valuable lesson. Shotton does not simply cite a 1970s laboratory finding and move on — the habit that embarrassed a decade of pop psychology. He reruns the classics as cheap modern tests: does charm pricing still move real shoppers? Does social-proof wording still shift real claims? Sometimes the old effect holds; sometimes it needs conditions. The implicit instruction to the reader is the book's ethic in one line: don't trust the lore — including this book's — test it on your own audience before you bet the budget. Published exactly as the replication crisis was humbling behavioural science, that stance aged better than almost anything else in the genre.

The standout chapters earn their fame. The pratfall effect: from the classic experiment where a capable person who spills coffee becomes more likeable, Shotton draws advertising's most counterintuitive tradition — campaigns built on admitted flaws. The ugly car, the reassuringly expensive lager, the second-place rental firm that tries harder: a brand that concedes something must be telling the truth about the rest, so imperfection, honestly deployed, outperforms polish. The boundary matters — the admitted flaw must be peripheral, the competence signal strong — and the chapter doubles as the book's own ethic: the honest version sells better anyway. The context chapters carry the deeper redirection: the heavy-lifting biases are environmental, not verbal. When and how an offer arrives — the anchor beside it, the queue implied behind it, the mood of the moment, the default it sits in — moves more than any copy tweak. Marketing effort, the book keeps showing, is misallocated toward wordsmithing when the leverage is in architecture.

And the book has a combative streak that made it a trade-press event: it asks fashionable industry orthodoxies for their evidence. The trade headline said it plainly — Shotton's Choice Factory brutally dismantles brand purpose — and the effectiveness wing of advertising adopted the book as its manual. Marketing professor Mark Ritson's documented verdict: a short but powerful tome from which you can learn how marketing actually influences consumers. Rory Sutherland — whose Alchemy is this book's flamboyant sibling — called it a Haynes manual for understanding consumer behaviour, and the pairing is exactly right: Sutherland supplies the magic, Shotton supplies the evidence, and together they are the advertising bookshelf's id and superego.

The honest ledger. The bias roster is the standard set — readers of Cialdini and Kahneman will recognise nearly everything, freshened by the field tests and the ad-industry case files. The chapters are deliberately thin: eight pages each means a scene, a study, and a page of advice, with boundary conditions mostly left as an exercise. The quick panel tests are directional screens, not journal science, and the classic corpus the book deals in varies in how well it survived the replication era — a problem the test-first method answers in principle and applies unevenly in practice. And a consultant popularising the toolkit he sells has an interest the text never audits. Judged on content: six point three out of ten, worthwhile — the highest applicable-ideas-per-minute on the persuasion shelf, below the top tier on depth and novelty.

So: read the full book if you market, price, or sell anything — it is an afternoon's read and a permanent desk reference, built for dipping. Stop here if you wanted the method and the standouts: test before trusting, context beats copy, flaws admitted sell. And run the book's own move this week: take one bias — say, charm pricing — and design the five-minute test that would check it on your actual audience. That habit, not any single bias, is what Shotton is really selling, and it is the one thing in the book guaranteed to replicate.
