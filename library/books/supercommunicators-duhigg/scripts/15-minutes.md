Charles Duhigg had a strange problem for a professional communicator. He had won a Pulitzer Prize as a New York Times journalist and written The Power of Habit. He explained complicated things to millions of people for a living. Then his first management job exposed how often he missed the human conversation beneath schedules and logistics. A similar failure appeared at home. On family holidays, Duhigg would complain because he wanted support; his wife would offer a sensible suggestion, and he would feel unheard. Supercommunicators, published by Random House in 2024, is his reported attempt to understand what was going wrong, and the sharper question underneath: what are the people who seem able to connect with almost anyone actually doing that the rest of us are not?

His answer starts with a taxonomy, and the taxonomy is the book. Talk moves among three kinds of conversation, Duhigg argues, often without anyone noticing which mode is active. The practical conversation asks: what is this really about? It wants to decide, plan, and solve — pick the school, fix the budget, choose the strategy. The emotional conversation asks: how do we feel? It wants feelings spoken and acknowledged, and it often does not want them solved; a solution offered mid-feeling can register as a refusal to listen. The social conversation asks: who are we? It negotiates identities — who we are to each other, which groups we belong to, how we want to be seen. The critical fact is that one discussion can move among all three. "We need to talk about your spending" can be a budget problem, a fear being voiced, or an accusation about what kind of person you are. On those holidays, Duhigg was opening an emotional conversation. His wife joined a practical one. Two competent communicators, in different modes, each concluding the other was not listening. Once you have the frame, you see the same collision everywhere: the doctor giving statistics to a frightened patient, the manager solving a complaint that was actually about respect, the parent negotiating logistics with a teenager who is asking who am I to you.

From the taxonomy comes the book's operating rule, the matching principle: connection happens when people are having the same kind of conversation at the same time. Supercommunicators are not defined by charisma. They are diagnostic. They notice which conversation is happening, join it rather than the one they had planned, and — the underrated move — negotiate openly about which conversation to have: do you want my thoughts, or do you need to vent first? Is this a decision we are making, or a worry we are sharing? The negotiation sounds clinical written down and lands as care in practice, because it lets the other person help set the agenda. In Duhigg's examples, effective communicators ask more questions, follow up on answers, and adjust when another person's mood or conversational goal changes. Duhigg garnishes the principle with neuroscience: successful conversation, he reports, can bring heart rates and breathing into alignment, alongside patterns of neural activity. The honest note is that this framing is oversold relative to a young and contested literature — pop treatments of neural coupling run well ahead of what the studies establish — but nothing practical hangs on it. The instruction survives without the brain scans: stop replying to the conversation you prepared, and join the one you are in.

The practical section adds a point that the three-label shorthand can hide: every conversation is also a negotiation about what people want, what belongs in the discussion, and how a decision will be made. Duhigg recommends preparing for an important exchange by writing down a couple of possible topics, one thing you hope to say, and one question you want to ask. The notes may never appear. Their value is making the purpose visible before anxiety and improvisation take over. Once the discussion begins, ask open questions about beliefs, values, and experience, then watch for whether the other person is reasoning through costs and benefits or through stories and similarities. The Leroy Reed jury shows why this matters. John Boly first broadened the agenda with questions about justice, then used empathy with some jurors and practical logic with the last holdout. Matching is not merely matching emotion. It includes matching what the other person will recognise as a reason.

The techniques are the book's working core, and their virtue is that they are procedures, not aspirations. The first is looping for understanding, borrowed from mediation practice. Three steps: ask a question; summarise what you heard in your own words; then check — did I get that right? The loop does two jobs at once. It forces genuine attention, because you cannot summarise what you did not hear. And it proves the attention to the speaker — which matters because listening that cannot be seen does no relational work. People do not open up because you listened; they open up because they saw you listen. The second technique is the deep question: reword facts into values. Not "where do you live?" but "what do you like about where you live?" Not "what do you do?" but "what made you choose that work?" A fact question invites a database lookup; a deep question invites the person to say something about what they care about. The Fast Friends procedure uses increasingly personal questions and reciprocal answers to help strangers form closeness quickly. Reciprocity is essential: if only one person discloses, the procedure does not build the same connection. Duhigg also shows how badly we predict these exchanges. People expect emotional questions to feel awkward, yet participants at the hedge-fund exercise he follows become absorbed in conversations they had dreaded. Looping has a companion misconception worth clearing: proving you understand is not agreeing. You can loop a position you intend to argue against — in fact that is when looping matters most, because people cannot hear a counter-argument from someone they believe has not heard them. Understanding first is not a concession; it is the price of admission to the disagreement. The third cluster is for screens. Text strips tone, face, and timing — the channels matching depends on. The compensations are unglamorous and effective: be more polite than feels necessary, cut the sarcasm, add gratitude and hedges, and never criticise in front of an audience, because an audience converts disagreement into performance.

The case studies are how the book argues, and they are its pleasure. CIA officer Jim Lawler spends months failing to recruit a reluctant woman with incentives and logic. The recruitment turns when he joins her emotional and social conversation about fear, loyalty, and identity, then shares vulnerability of his own. In the Leroy Reed jury, John Boly does not simply repeat arguments. He asks open questions about justice and handguns, broadens what the discussion is about, then changes between empathetic stories and practical logic to match different jurors. Surgeon Behfar Ehdaie learns to ask prostate-cancer patients what the diagnosis means to them before assuming they only want facts; some need data, others reassurance or a discussion about family. The Big Bang Theory illustrates emotional matching: characters make their feelings legible to viewers by mirroring one another's mood and energy, or create tension by failing to match. NASA psychiatrist Terrance McGuire tests a similar skill by laughing after pretending to drop papers and watching whether astronaut candidates match the room's mood and energy. Doctors facing vaccine-hesitant parents learn that facts alone miss a social-identity conversation. Netflix, after a public firing over a racial slur, tries to make conversations about race and belonging safer. And in facilitated gun-control dialogues, opponents use looping to prove they heard one another. They do not converge. They keep talking. That, the book is honest enough to show, is sometimes the ceiling.

The vaccine case makes the social mode concrete. Dr. Rosenbloom first tries authority and more information with hesitant parents. Both approaches fail because the conflict is partly between identities: independent, sceptical parents on one side; medical experts on the other. He changes his own view of the parents, uses motivational interviewing, and looks for identities they share, such as being fathers or sending children to the same schools. Duhigg pairs this with Salma Mousa's football study in post-ISIS Iraq. Christian teams were assigned Muslim players, given equal-playing rules, and encouraged to cooperate. The shared identity of teammate improved behaviour inside the league, though the effects did not erase prejudice everywhere else. That boundary matters. A common identity can open contact in a designed setting; it is not evidence that one conversation dissolves a wider conflict.

The identity chapters deserve their own note, because they name the conversation type many workplaces struggle to hold. Who-are-we conversations — race, belief, belonging, status — fail differently from the other two kinds: under identity threat, people become defensive and stop hearing the discussion in the same way. The book's advice is structural rather than heroic. Prepare, establish guidelines, state goals, acknowledge that discomfort is normal, draw out people's multiple identities, and keep participants on equal footing. Netflix changed its loose culture of spontaneous debate by adding guidance for conversations about bias: favour curiosity and personal experience, avoid blame, and prioritise respect. It is the section where the book is most useful and most obviously incomplete at the same time. What works in a facilitated room with goodwill is not a plan for a polarised country. It also assumes both sides can safely keep talking; coercion can make withdrawal the wiser response. The modest contribution is a procedure for making some difficult conversations discussable at all.

Now the critical ledger, because the reception splits cleanly and both halves are right. The mainstream verdict: warm. Best-of-year listings, a business-book award shortlist, and the trade reviews' judgement that Duhigg unpacks the essential tools for effective conversations — actionable, humane, well organised. The critical verdict, put sharply by one professional reviewer: the advice is rather obvious once stated. Listen deeply, ask questions, be authentic — the moves are renamed from a half-century of active-listening literature, from Rogers through mediation training, and readers of that canon will find refinement here, not discovery. The ethics get little attention: these are persuasion skills, the same ones a manipulator would want, and the book never seriously asks when deep questions become extraction, or what accountability looks like for skilled persuaders. The online chapter is etiquette for a problem that is often adversarial — harassment and coordinated bad faith do not yield to hedges and gratitude. And throughout, the evidence arrives as stories: real studies, honestly reported, never quantified, so the reader cannot tell the robust findings from the fragile ones.

Supercommunicators rates six out of ten: worthwhile. Explanatory power is good — the three-conversations frame genuinely explains a common failure while it is happening. Practical utility is the strength — looping, question rewording, and the online rules can be used the day you hear them, which is rarer in this genre than it should be. Evidence is the dock: familiar science, narrated rather than measured, with a neural garnish it does not need. Calibration is the deeper dock: the framework's limits — bad faith, power gaps, scale — go mostly unexamined. Efficiency is respectable: three hundred and twenty pages, the frame fully delivered early, the rest illustration that mostly earns its space.

Read the full book if case stories are how you learn, or if you want the techniques rehearsed in enough narrative depth to survive contact with a real argument — it is a fast, warm, well-built read, and the jury, CIA, and Netflix chapters are genuinely instructive. Skip it if you only want the framework, because that transfers completely: three conversations, match before you reply, loop until they say exactly, one values question a day, and four rules for the screen. It is also a poor fit if you know the listening canon — Nonviolent Communication, Difficult Conversations, motivational interviewing — or if your hard conversations are with people arguing in bad faith, because this book assumes goodwill on both sides of the table.

Either way, keep the diagnostic, because it costs nothing and starts working immediately. Tonight, when someone wants to complain, hold the fix. Ask whether they want to be helped, hugged, or heard — adapting the words to the setting. That half-second of diagnosis before response is the entire book in miniature. The people we call naturals were never simply born that way. They check which conversation they are in, show that they heard it, and then speak clearly.
