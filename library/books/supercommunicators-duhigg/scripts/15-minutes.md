Charles Duhigg had a strange problem for a professional communicator. He had won a Pulitzer Prize as a New York Times journalist. He had written The Power of Habit, one of the bestselling behavioural books of its decade. He explained complicated things to millions of people for a living. And at home, his conversations kept misfiring in the same place. His wife would come home and describe a terrible day — a boss who talked over her, a meeting that went sideways — and Duhigg would do the obvious helpful thing: offer solutions. Have you thought about saying this? What if you emailed him that? And she would get angrier — at the day, and now at him. He was being genuinely useful. It was making everything worse. Supercommunicators, published by Random House in 2024, is his reported attempt to understand what he was doing wrong, and the sharper question underneath: what are the people who never seem to misfire — the colleague everyone confides in, the negotiator both sides trust, the friend who can talk to anyone — actually doing that the rest of us are not?

His answer starts with a taxonomy, and the taxonomy is the book. Every discussion, Duhigg argues, is really one of three conversations, usually without anyone noticing which. The practical conversation asks: what is this really about? It wants to decide, plan, and solve — pick the school, fix the budget, choose the strategy. The emotional conversation asks: how do we feel? It wants feelings spoken and acknowledged, and it specifically does not want them solved; a solution offered mid-feeling registers as a refusal to listen. The social conversation asks: who are we? It negotiates identities — who we are to each other, which groups we belong to, how we want to be seen. The critical fact about the three is that the same sentence can belong to any of them. "We need to talk about your spending" can be a budget discussion, a fear being voiced, or an accusation about what kind of person you are — and which one it is exists in the speaker's head, not in the words. When Duhigg's wife described her day, she was opening an emotional conversation. He kept joining a practical one. Two competent communicators, in different conversations, each concluding the other wasn't listening. Once you have the frame, you see the same collision everywhere: the doctor giving statistics to a frightened patient, the manager solving a complaint that was actually about respect, the parent negotiating logistics with a teenager who is asking who am I to you.

From the taxonomy comes the book's operating rule, the matching principle: connection happens when people are having the same kind of conversation at the same time. Supercommunicators are not charismatic, Duhigg insists — in the book's studies of who groups actually confide in, they are often the quietest people in the room. They are diagnostic. They notice which conversation is happening, join it rather than the one they had planned, and — the underrated move — negotiate openly about which conversation to have: do you want my thoughts, or do you need to vent first? Is this a decision we are making, or a worry we are sharing? The negotiation sounds clinical written down and lands as care in practice, because it tells the other person their conversation, not yours, sets the agenda. Duhigg's studies of real groups keep finding the same signature: the person everyone rates as easiest to talk to asks far more questions than average, follows up on the answers, and switches conversational registers the moment their counterpart does — matching so habitual it looks like personality. Duhigg garnishes the principle with neuroscience: successful conversation, he reports, shows up in the lab as synchrony, two brains' activity falling into alignment, pupils dilating together, speech rhythms entraining. The honest note is that this framing is oversold relative to a young and contested literature — pop treatments of neural coupling run well ahead of what the studies establish — but nothing practical hangs on it. The instruction survives without the brain scans: stop replying to the conversation you prepared, and join the one you are in.

The techniques are the book's working core, and their virtue is that they are procedures, not aspirations. The first is looping for understanding, borrowed from mediation practice. Three steps: ask a question; summarise what you heard in your own words; then check — did I get that right? The loop does two jobs at once. It forces genuine attention, because you cannot summarise what you did not hear. And it proves the attention to the speaker — which matters because listening that cannot be seen does no relational work. People do not open up because you listened; they open up because they saw you listen. The second technique is the deep question: reword facts into values. Not "where do you live?" but "what do you like about where you live?" Not "what do you do?" but "what made you choose that work?" A fact question invites a database lookup; a deep question invites the person to say something about what they care about — and, in the research tradition the book draws on, escalating mutual self-disclosure is the most reliable closeness-manufacturing machine psychology has found. Vulnerability, reciprocated, is the currency. The finding Duhigg leans on is how badly we predict this: people expect deep questions to strangers to be awkward, and in study after study both sides enjoy them more than small talk, remember the exchange longer, and want to repeat it. Looping has a companion misconception worth clearing: proving you understand is not agreeing. You can loop a position you intend to argue against — in fact that is when looping matters most, because people cannot hear a counter-argument from someone they believe has not heard them. Understanding first is not a concession; it is the price of admission to the disagreement. The third cluster is for screens. Text strips tone, face, and timing — the channels matching depends on — so receivers systematically read digital messages as colder and more hostile than intended. The compensations are unglamorous and effective: be more polite than feels necessary, cut the sarcasm, add gratitude and hedges, and never criticise in front of an audience, because an audience converts disagreement into performance.

The case studies are how the book argues, and they are its pleasure. A CIA officer spends months failing to recruit a foreign official with incentives and logic — practical conversation — and the recruitment turns only when he joins the conversation the man is actually having, an emotional one about fear, loyalty, and what betrayal would make of him. A deadlocked jury moves when one juror, instead of arguing, loops: he restates each holdout's position until its owner says exactly — and only then does the room begin to reason together. The writers' room of The Big Bang Theory spends its energy negotiating what kind of show they are making — which is a social conversation about what kind of room they are. A surgeon stops presenting a frightened patient with treatment options and asks what he wants his remaining years to actually look like; the treatment decision changes on the spot. Netflix, after a public firing over a racial slur, tries to have the company-wide conversation about race and belonging that most organisations avoid — and progress comes, in Duhigg's telling, only when the rules are made explicit before the content: what participants may ask, how mistakes will be treated, whether people speak as themselves or as representatives. Doctors facing vaccine-hesitant patients stop reciting evidence — a practical conversation aimed at an emotional and social one — and start asking what the patient is actually afraid of, which does not always change minds but changes what kind of conversation is possible. And in facilitated gun-control dialogues, trained opponents loop each other's positions until each side can state the other's case to its owner's satisfaction. They do not converge. They keep talking. That, the book is honest enough to show, is sometimes the ceiling — and the marriage-counselling material makes the same point at kitchen-table scale: couples who fight well are not couples who agree, but couples who prove understanding before they argue, and who fight about the issue instead of about whether the other person is listening.

The identity chapters deserve their own note, because they name the conversation type most workplaces are currently failing at. Who-are-we conversations — race, belief, belonging, status — fail differently from the other two kinds: under identity threat, people stop examining claims and start defending groups. The book's advice is structural rather than heroic: acknowledge that everyone in the room has multiple identities, not just the one under discussion; distinguish intent from impact explicitly; set the conversation's rules before its content. It is the section where the book is most useful and most obviously incomplete at the same time — what works in a facilitated room with goodwill is not a plan for a polarised country, and the book knows it. But the workplace version is real: most organisations currently run their hardest conversations as practical ones — policies, trainings, statements — precisely because the social conversation underneath feels unmanageable, and the book's modest contribution is a procedure for making it discussable at all.

Now the critical ledger, because the reception splits cleanly and both halves are right. The mainstream verdict: warm. Best-of-year listings, a business-book award shortlist, and the trade reviews' judgement that Duhigg unpacks the essential tools for effective conversations — actionable, humane, well organised. The critical verdict, put sharply by one professional reviewer: the advice is rather obvious once stated. Listen deeply, ask questions, be authentic — the moves are renamed from a half-century of active-listening literature, from Rogers through mediation training, and readers of that canon will find refinement here, not discovery. The ethics get little attention: these are persuasion skills, the same ones a manipulator would want, and the book never seriously asks when deep questions become extraction, or what accountability looks like for skilled persuaders. The online chapter is etiquette for a problem that is often adversarial — harassment and coordinated bad faith do not yield to hedges and gratitude. And throughout, the evidence arrives as stories: real studies, honestly reported, never quantified, so the reader cannot tell the robust findings from the fragile ones.

Judged on content alone, as this library judges, Supercommunicators rates six out of ten: worthwhile. The arithmetic: explanatory power is good — the three-conversations frame genuinely explains a common failure while it is happening. Practical utility is the strength — looping, question rewording, and the online rules can be used the day you hear them, which is rarer in this genre than it should be. Evidence is the dock: familiar science, narrated rather than measured, with a neural garnish it does not need. Calibration is the deeper dock: the framework's limits — bad faith, power gaps, scale — go mostly unexamined. Efficiency is respectable: three hundred and twenty pages, the frame fully delivered early, the rest illustration that mostly earns its space.

So, the decision. Read the full book if case stories are how you learn, or if you want the techniques rehearsed in enough narrative depth to survive contact with a real argument — it is a fast, warm, well-built read, and the jury, CIA, and Netflix chapters are genuinely instructive. Stop here if you wanted the framework, because it transfers completely: three conversations, match before you reply, loop until they say exactly, one values question a day, and four rules for the screen. Skip it if you know the listening canon — Nonviolent Communication, Difficult Conversations, motivational interviewing — or if your hard conversations are with people arguing in bad faith, because this book assumes goodwill on both sides of the table.

Either way, keep the diagnostic, because it costs nothing and starts working immediately. Tonight, when someone you live with describes a bad day, hold the fix. Ask instead whether they want help, or company, or just to be heard — and watch what the question does to their face. That half-second of diagnosis before response is the entire book in miniature. Duhigg spent three hundred pages and dozens of case studies earning it, road-tested it in his own marriage, and reports that the misfires that started the book now mostly end differently. The people we call naturals were never naturals. They just check which conversation they are in before they speak — and now, so can you.
