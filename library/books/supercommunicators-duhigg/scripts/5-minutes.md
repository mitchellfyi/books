Charles Duhigg had a strange problem for a professional communicator. A Pulitzer-winning journalist, author of The Power of Habit, a man paid to explain things — and his conversations at home kept misfiring. His wife would describe a terrible day at work, he would offer sensible solutions, and she would get angrier. He was being helpful. It was making everything worse. Supercommunicators, published in 2024, is his reported answer to what he was doing wrong, and to the question underneath: what are the people who never misfire — the colleague everyone confides in, the friend who can talk to anyone — actually doing?

The book's central claim is that every discussion is really one of three conversations, and most failure is a mismatch between them. The practical conversation asks: what is this really about — what are we deciding or solving? The emotional conversation asks: how do we feel — and wants feelings acknowledged, not fixed. The social conversation asks: who are we — which identities are in the room? The same sentence can belong to any of the three. When his wife described her day, she was opening an emotional conversation; Duhigg kept answering with a practical one. Both people were communicating competently — in different conversations.

From this comes what he calls the matching principle: connection happens when people are having the same kind of conversation at the same time. Supercommunicators are not charismatic; they are diagnostic. They notice which conversation is actually happening, join it, and negotiate openly about which one to have next. Duhigg dresses the point in neuroscience — brains falling into sync during successful conversation — and the garnish is oversold relative to a young literature, but the practical instruction survives without it: stop replying to the conversation you planned and join the one you are in.

The techniques are the book's working core, and they are learnable this afternoon. Looping for understanding: ask a question, summarise the answer in your own words, then check — did I get that right? It forces real attention and, just as important, proves it; listening that cannot be seen might as well not exist. Deep questions: reword facts into values. Not "where do you live?" but "what do you like about where you live?" Not "what do you do?" but "what did you want to do when you were a kid?" People answer deep questions gladly, vulnerability invites vulnerability, and closeness turns out to be less a mystery than a staircase. And for the screen, where all the nonverbal channels are stripped away, four compensating rules: be more polite than feels necessary, drop the sarcasm, add gratitude and hedges, and never criticise in public.

The case studies carry the argument. A CIA officer fails to recruit a reluctant woman until he stops pitching — a practical conversation — and joins her real conversation, which is emotional: fear, loyalty, what betrayal would make of her. A deadlocked jury moves when one juror loops the others until each side can state the opposing case to its owner's satisfaction. The writers' room of The Big Bang Theory negotiates what kind of show — what kind of conversation — they are making. A surgeon stops listing treatment options and asks a patient what he wants his remaining life to look like, and the decision changes. Netflix tries to talk about race and belonging after a public firing, and progress comes only when the conversation's rules are made explicit before its content. Gun-control opponents in facilitated dialogues do not converge — but they keep talking, which is the book's honest ceiling.

That ceiling is where the criticism lives, and it is fair. The advice, one professional reviewer noted, is rather obvious once stated — listen, ask, be genuine — and readers of the active-listening classics will recognise renamed moves. The ethics get little attention: these are persuasion skills, and the book never really asks what happens when they are used to extract rather than connect. The online chapter is etiquette for a problem that is often adversarial. And the toolkit visibly thins at scale — what works in a facilitated room is not a plan for a polarised country. Judged on content, it rates six out of ten: worthwhile — one genuinely clarifying distinction, high practical utility, familiar science.

Read the full book if case stories are how you learn — it is fast, warm, and well built. Skip it if you only want the framework, because that transfers completely: three conversations, match before you reply, loop until they say "exactly," and ask one values question a day. Tonight, when someone describes their bad day, try the diagnosis before the fix. Ask whether they want to be helped, hugged, or heard. That one question is the book in miniature — and it works.
