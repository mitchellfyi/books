Charles Duhigg had a strange problem for a professional communicator. A Pulitzer-winning journalist and author of The Power of Habit, he was paid to explain things, yet he struggled with the human side of his first management job. At home, he would complain during a family holiday because he wanted support; his wife would offer sensible advice, and he would feel unheard. Supercommunicators, published in 2024, is his reported answer to what was going wrong, and to the question underneath: what are people who seem able to connect with almost anyone actually doing?

The book's central claim is that talk moves among three kinds of conversation, and many failures are mismatches between them. The practical conversation asks: what is this really about — what are we deciding or solving? The emotional conversation asks: how do we feel — and wants feelings acknowledged, not fixed. The social conversation asks: who are we — which identities are in the room? The same sentence can belong to any of the three. On those holidays, Duhigg was opening an emotional conversation; his wife answered a practical one. Both were communicating competently in different modes.

From this comes what he calls the matching principle: connection happens when people are having the same kind of conversation at the same time. Supercommunicators are not charismatic; they are diagnostic. They notice which conversation is actually happening, join it, and negotiate openly about which one to have next. Duhigg dresses the point in neuroscience — brains falling into sync during successful conversation — and the garnish is oversold relative to a young literature, but the practical instruction survives without it: stop replying to the conversation you planned and join the one you are in.

The techniques are the book's working core, and they are learnable this afternoon. Looping for understanding: ask a question, summarise the answer in your own words, then check — did I get that right? It forces real attention and, just as important, proves it; listening that cannot be seen might as well not exist. Deep questions: reword facts into values. Not "where do you live?" but "what do you like about where you live?" Not "what do you do?" but "what did you want to do when you were a kid?" People answer deep questions gladly, vulnerability invites vulnerability, and closeness turns out to be less a mystery than a staircase. And for the screen, where all the nonverbal channels are stripped away, four compensating rules: be more polite than feels necessary, drop the sarcasm, add gratitude and hedges, and never criticise in public.

The case studies carry the argument. A CIA officer fails to recruit a reluctant woman until he stops pitching — a practical conversation — and joins her emotional conversation about fear, loyalty, and identity. In the Leroy Reed jury, John Boly asks open questions, broadens what the discussion is about, then matches logical and empathetic arguments to different jurors. Surgeon Behfar Ehdaie learns to ask prostate-cancer patients what the diagnosis means to them before assuming they only want medical facts. The Big Bang Theory illustrates how matching mood and energy lets viewers read emotion. Netflix tries to talk about race and belonging after a public firing, while gun-control opponents use looping to keep a difficult conversation going without pretending they agree.

That ceiling is where the criticism lives, and it is fair. The advice, one professional reviewer noted, is rather obvious once stated — listen, ask, be genuine — and readers of the active-listening classics will recognise renamed moves. The ethics get little attention: these are persuasion skills, and the book never really asks what happens when they are used to extract rather than connect. The online chapter is etiquette for a problem that is often adversarial. And the toolkit visibly thins at scale — what works in a facilitated room is not a plan for a polarised country. Judged on content, it rates six point one out of ten: worthwhile — one genuinely clarifying distinction, high practical utility, familiar science.

Read the full book if case stories are how you learn — it is fast, warm, and well built. Skip it if you only want the framework, because that transfers completely: three conversations, match before you reply, loop until they say "exactly," and ask one values question a day. Tonight, when someone describes their bad day, try the diagnosis before the fix. Ask whether they want to be helped, hugged, or heard. That one question is the book in miniature — and it works.
